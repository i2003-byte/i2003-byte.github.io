📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A triangle with two equal sides is called isosceles. Fold it along the line that halves the angle between the equal sides, and one half lies exactly on the other. So the two angles opposite the equal sides, the base angles, must be equal.

The other way round is true too: if two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite them are equal. When the sides are not equal, the fold misses, and the longer side always faces the bigger angle.

🧮 Key rules

AB = AC ⇒ ∠B = ∠C

Proof: draw AD, the bisector of ∠A. In ΔABD and ΔACD, AB = AC, ∠BAD = ∠CAD and AD is common, so ΔABD ≅ ΔACD by SAS. Hence ∠B = ∠C (corresponding parts, CPCT).

∠B = ∠C ⇒ AB = AC

The converse. Proof: with the same bisector, ΔABD ≅ ΔACD by AAS (∠B = ∠C, ∠BAD = ∠CAD, AD common).

The same congruence also gives BD = DC and ∠ADB = ∠ADC = 90°: in an isosceles triangle, the bisector of the top angle is also the perpendicular bisector of the base. If all three sides are equal, every angle is 180° ÷ 3 = 60°.

In the sim, D is where the bisector of ∠A meets BC. Folding turns the flap ADC over the line AD like paper; C ends up on line AB, at the same distance from A as before.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • The two slopes of a village hut roof or a temple gopuram front are usually equal, so the roof makes equal angles with the walls on both sides.
  • A coat hanger, a ladder opened into an A-shape, and the two equal ropes of a swing hanging from one point all make isosceles triangles.
  • A kite (patang) is two isosceles triangles joined base to base; folding it along its spine shows the same symmetry.

📖 Key terms

  • Isosceles triangle: a triangle with (at least) two equal sides.
  • Base angles: the two angles opposite the equal sides.
  • Angle bisector: a line that cuts an angle into two equal angles.
  • Converse: a statement with the “if” and “then” parts swapped.
