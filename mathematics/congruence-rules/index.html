📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Two triangles are congruent if one can be placed exactly on top of the other, perhaps after turning it or flipping it over. Then all six parts match: three sides and three angles.

You do not need to check all six. If three well-chosen parts are given, a ruler and compass can draw only one triangle from them, so any two triangles built from those parts must be congruent. That is what a congruence rule means. Some choices of three parts, though, leave room for two different triangles, or for any size.

🧮 Key rules

SSS · SAS · ASA (and AAS) · RHS

Three sides; two sides and the angle between them; two angles and the side between them (or any side, since the third angle is 180° minus the other two); in right triangles, the hypotenuse and one other side.

ΔABC ≅ ΔPQR means A↔P, B↔Q, C↔R

The order of the letters tells you which parts match: AB = PQ, ∠B = ∠Q and so on. CPCT: corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.

Not rules: SSA (two sides and an angle that is not between them) can give two different triangles, the “ambiguous case”: the arc from B cuts the ray from A twice. AAA fixes only the shape: triangles with equal angles are similar but can be any size.

In the sim, the base AB is drawn first and C is found where the compass arcs (dashed circles) and the angle rays meet. Lengths are in cm and rounded to 0.1 cm.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A carpenter cuts three bars and joins them into a triangle: it cannot change shape (SSS). That is why roof trusses, electric towers and bridges are built from triangles, while a four-bar frame wobbles.
  • To make many identical triangular kites or rangoli tiles, you only need to copy two sides and the angle between them (SAS).
  • A surveyor can find a far-off point, like a tree across a river, by measuring a base line and two angles at its ends (ASA).

🎯 Try this

  1. Choose SSS with sides 7, 5 and 6 cm and tap “Test with a friend’s copy”. The friend’s triangle starts turned over. Does it still fit?
    Show answerYes. Turned and flipped back, it lands exactly on yours. Three sides allow only one triangle, so the two are congruent.
  2. In SSS, set AB = 9 cm, CA = 3 cm and BC = 4 cm. What happens to the arcs?
    Show answerThey do not meet: 3 + 4 = 7 cm cannot reach across 9 cm, so no triangle exists. Any two sides must add up to more than the third.
  3. Choose SSA with AB = 7 cm, ∠A = 50° and BC = 6 cm. How many triangles fit? Now test with a friend’s copy.
    Show answerTwo: the arc from B cuts the ray at C₁ and C₂. The friend builds the other one, and it does not fit on yours, even though the three given parts are the same. So SSA is not a congruence rule.
  4. Still in SSA, make BC longer than AB (say 8 cm). How many triangles now?
    Show answerOnly one, because the second crossing falls behind A. SSA only works by luck, which is why it is not a rule. RHS is the safe special case: when the given angle is 90°, there is always just one.
  5. Choose AAA with ∠A = 60° and ∠B = 60° and test with a friend’s copy. What goes wrong?
    Show answerBoth are equilateral, with the same angles, but the friend’s is smaller. Equal angles fix the shape, not the size.

📖 Key terms

  • Congruent (≅): exactly the same shape and size.
  • Included angle: the angle between two given sides.
  • Hypotenuse: the side opposite the right angle, the longest side of a right triangle.
  • Similar: the same shape but possibly a different size.
