📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Survey questions give you totals that overlap. “400 read Hindi” includes the 150 who read both Hindi and English, so you cannot just add the numbers. A Venn diagram keeps the overlaps apart: every person sits in exactly one region.

The trick is to start in the middle, where the most circles overlap, because that number is usually given directly. Then work outwards: each next region is a given total minus the regions already filled inside it. When every region is filled, any question (only one, exactly two, at least one, none) is just adding the right regions.

🧮 Key formulas

n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B) n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A ∩ B) − n(B ∩ C) − n(C ∩ A) + n(A ∩ B ∩ C)

Adding the three circles counts each pair overlap twice, so subtract the pairs. But that removes the middle once too often, so add it back.

none = total − n(A ∪ B ∪ C)
  • Only A and B (not C) = n(A ∩ B) − n(A ∩ B ∩ C).
  • Only A = n(A) − (the three other regions inside circle A).
  • Every region must come out as zero or more. A negative region means the survey numbers contradict each other.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Newspaper companies survey a colony to see how many homes take two papers, so they know how many readers an advert in both papers really reaches.
  • A school planning sports periods needs to know how many students play more than one game, so that matches don’t clash.
  • Health surveys count people with diabetes, high blood pressure, or both; adding the two totals would count the “both” group twice.

📖 Key terms

  • At least one: in one or more of the sets, the union.
  • Exactly one / only one: in one set and none of the others.
  • Inclusion–exclusion: the add-subtract-add-back formula for the size of a union.
