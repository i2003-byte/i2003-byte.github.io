📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

After following one trait at a time, Mendel asked a bolder question: if a plant differs in two traits, are they passed on together, or separately? He crossed pure-breeding plants with round yellow seeds (RRYY) with pure plants that had wrinkled green seeds (rryy).

  • All the F1 seeds were round and yellow (RrYy). Round and yellow are both dominant.
  • When the F1 plants self-pollinated, the F2 had four kinds of seeds, including two new mixes that neither grandparent had: round green and wrinkled yellow.
  • Law of independent assortment: the alleles for seed shape and the alleles for seed colour go into gametes separately. A RrYy plant makes four kinds of gametes, RY, Ry, rY and ry, in equal numbers.

🧮 The 4 × 4 Punnett square

Four kinds of egg cells meet four kinds of pollen, so the square has 16 equally likely boxes, each ¹⁄₁₆.

RrYy × RrYy → 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green P(round yellow) = P(round) × P(yellow) = ¾ × ¾ = 9⁄16 test cross: RrYy × rryy → 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

Look at one gene at a time and the old 3 : 1 is still there: 12 round : 4 wrinkled, and 12 yellow : 4 green. The 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is just (3 : 1) × (3 : 1).

Simplified: complete dominance, every gamete equally likely, and the two genes on different chromosomes. Genes that sit close together on the same chromosome are linked and tend to travel together, so they break the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 rule (you meet linkage in Class 12). Small batches wander away from the expected ratio by chance.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Mendel's own F2 count was 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green, out of 556 seeds. Scaled to 16 that is 9.1 : 3.1 : 2.9 : 0.9, very close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  • Breeders use independent assortment to put two good traits into one variety. For example, crossing a high-yielding wheat with a disease-resistant one and picking the rare F2 plants that have both, then breeding them until they are pure.
  • Mendel was lucky: the seven pea traits he studied behave almost independently. Had he picked two linked genes, his numbers would not have fitted this neat pattern.

🎯 Try this

  1. Press “Pure × pure”, then Grow seeds. What do all the F1 seeds look like, and what is their genotype?
    Show answerAll are round and yellow, and every box in the square is RrYy. Each child gets RY from one parent and ry from the other.
  2. Press “Self the F1” and set Highlight to “Wrinkled green boxes”. How many of the 16 boxes light up?
    Show answerJust one, rryy. A seed is wrinkled green only if it gets ry from both parents: ¼ × ¼ = ¹⁄₁₆. Try round yellow next: 9 boxes.
  3. Set the number of seeds to 556 (Mendel's batch) and grow the F2. Compare “Observed, scaled to 16” with Mendel's count.
    Show answerBoth land near 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, but never exactly, and your run will differ from Mendel's and from the next run. That is chance at work. With 1600 seeds the numbers usually get closer.
  4. In the F2, look at the readouts “Shape alone” and “Colour alone”. What ratio do you see for each?
    Show answerAbout 3 : 1 for each. Seed shape and seed colour each follow the monohybrid rule on their own, which shows the two genes are sorted independently.
  5. A round yellow plant of unknown genotype is crossed with rryy. Its seeds come out round yellow and round green, half and half. What was its genotype?
    Show answerRRYy. All children are round, so it gives only R; half are green, so it carries one y. Check it by setting Parent 1 to RRYy and Parent 2 to rryy.

📖 Key terms

  • Dihybrid cross: a cross that follows two traits at once.
  • Independent assortment: alleles of different genes enter gametes independently of each other.
  • Recombinant: an offspring with a new mix of traits not seen in either original parent (round green, wrinkled yellow).
  • Test cross: crossing with a fully recessive plant (rryy) to reveal an unknown genotype.
  • Linkage: genes close together on one chromosome, which tend to be inherited together.
