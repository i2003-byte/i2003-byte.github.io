📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Every cell makes waste. Breaking down proteins makes urea, which is poisonous if it builds up. The two kidneys clean the blood. Each has about a million tiny filtering units called nephrons.

A nephron works in two steps. First the glomerulus, a knot of thin blood vessels inside a cup called Bowman's capsule, squeezes water, salt, glucose and urea out of the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too big and stay behind. Then, along the long tubule, everything useful is taken back into the blood: all the glucose, almost all the salt and about 99 % of the water. What is left, water with urea and extra salt, is urine. It flows through the ureters into the bladder.

How much water is taken back depends on the body. When you are short of water, a hormone (ADH) makes the collecting duct save more water, so you pass a little dark urine. When you drink a lot, the kidneys let the extra water go, and urine is pale.

🧮 The numbers

Filtered: about 180 L a day → urine: about 1–2 L a day

About 1.2 L of blood flows through the two kidneys every minute, and 125 mL a minute is filtered. So more than 99 % of the filtered water must go back into the blood.

urine = water in − water lost in other ways

Water in = what you drink + about 0.7 L in food + 0.3 L made by your cells. Other losses = about 0.8 L in breath and from the skin, sweat, and 0.1 L in faeces.

least urine ≈ waste to remove ÷ strongest urine ≈ 0.5 L a day

The kidneys cannot make urine stronger than a certain limit, so a little water must always leave with the waste. If you drink less than that, you start to dry out (dehydration) and feel thirsty.

sugar in urine (g/day) ≈ 1.8 × (blood sugar − 180 mg/dL)

The tubule can take back only so much glucose. Above about 180 mg/dL of blood sugar the rest spills into the urine, which is a sign of diabetes. The extra sugar needs water to carry it, so a person with high sugar passes more urine and feels thirsty.

Simplifications: one nephron stands for all two million, and the flow is shown hugely slowed down (one trip along the tubule takes about 9 s here). The shares taken back in each part of the tubule are rounded textbook values. The daily figures are rounded for an adult: 180 L filtered, 0.8 L lost in breath and from the skin, about 2 L of sweat on a hot day, a fixed amount of waste (600 mOsm) and a strongest urine of 1200 mOsm/L. Real kidneys also adjust salt, potassium and acid, which is not shown, and the glucose limit is gradual, not sharp. Urine colour also changes with food and medicines.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Summer in Delhi or Nagpur: you sweat litres a day, so you need to drink much more water, and urine becomes darker. Pale yellow urine is a simple sign that you are drinking enough.
  • Diabetes test: doctors and labs test urine for sugar. Healthy kidneys return all the glucose, so sugar in urine means the blood sugar is too high.
  • Dialysis: when both kidneys fail, a dialysis machine (artificial kidney) passes the blood through tubes with a thin membrane in a bath of fluid. Urea and extra salt diffuse out, but nothing is taken back, so it cannot copy the tubule.
  • Kidney stones are more common in hot, dry places; drinking enough water makes urine less concentrated and helps prevent them.

📖 Key terms

  • Excretion: removing harmful waste, such as urea, from the body.
  • Nephron: the filtering unit of the kidney: glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, tubule and collecting duct.
  • Filtration: small molecules pushed out of the blood in the glomerulus by blood pressure.
  • Reabsorption: useful substances (glucose, salt, water) taken back from the tubule into the blood.
  • ADH: a hormone that makes the kidney save water when the body is short of it.
