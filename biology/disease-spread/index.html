📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Many diseases are caused by microorganisms (pathogens): viruses cause flu, measles, chickenpox and dengue; bacteria cause typhoid and tuberculosis; a protozoan causes malaria. A disease that passes from one person to another is called a communicable disease.

Germs travel in different ways. Flu and measles spread through the air in tiny droplets when a sick person coughs or sneezes, so people nearby catch them. Dengue and malaria cannot pass straight from person to person: a mosquito picks up the germ when it bites a sick person and passes it on with its next bites. The mosquito is a carrier.

A vaccine trains the body to fight a germ before it ever meets it. A vaccinated person cannot catch the disease, and also cannot pass it on, so vaccines protect the people around them too.

🧮 Key ideas

herd immunity needs 1 − 1/R₀ of people immune

R₀ (“R nought”) is how many people one sick person would infect in a crowd where nobody is immune. Flu-like: R₀ ≈ 2, so about half must be immune. Measles: R₀ about 12–18, so 92–95 % must be vaccinated.

Why an outbreak grows: if each sick person infects more than one other before they recover, the number of sick people keeps rising. When fewer than one new person is infected each time, it dies out.

Herd immunity: when enough people are immune, a sick person mostly meets people who cannot catch it, so the germ cannot find new hosts. Even the few who are not vaccinated (babies, or people too ill for a vaccine) stay safe.

Other protection: masks, covering a cough and washing hands cut the germs that reach others; sick people who stay at home meet fewer people. For mosquito diseases, nets and repellents stop bites, and emptying stagnant water (coolers, tyres, pots) stops mosquitoes breeding.

The sim is a simple model: 160 people walk at random; each cough can infect anyone healthy within a fixed distance with a fixed chance; everyone is sick for about 5 (measles-like: 7) days and then immune for good; vaccines work every time; mosquitoes bite someone nearby about once a day and live about 10 days. Nobody dies or is born. In a town this small an outbreak quickly runs short of people to infect, so even a measles-like germ shows fewer than 12 new cases per person. Each run is a little different, like real outbreaks. 1 second of the sim is 2 days.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • India’s Pulse Polio campaign vaccinated almost every child under 5, again and again, until polio was gone; India was declared polio-free in 2014.
  • The Universal Immunisation Programme gives free vaccines for measles, rubella, tuberculosis (BCG), diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus and more to millions of babies every year.
  • Dengue is spread by the Aedes mosquito, which breeds in clean still water and bites by day; malaria by the female Anopheles, which bites at night. Cases rise in and just after the monsoon.
  • Edward Jenner made the first vaccine (against smallpox) in 1796. Smallpox was wiped out across the world by 1980.

📖 Key terms

  • Pathogen: a microorganism that causes disease.
  • Communicable disease: a disease that spreads from one person to another, through air, water, food, touch or a carrier.
  • Carrier: an animal (like a mosquito or housefly) that carries germs from one person to another.
  • Vaccine: a harmless form of a germ (or part of it) that teaches the body to fight that germ.
  • Herd immunity: protection of a whole group because so many people in it are immune.
