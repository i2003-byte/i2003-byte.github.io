📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A leaf turns the glucose it makes in photosynthesis into starch and stores it. So if we find starch in a leaf, we know photosynthesis happened there. Iodine solution is the test: it turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown without it.

By keeping one part of a leaf away from light, using a leaf whose white parts have no chlorophyll, or taking away the carbon dioxide around a leaf, we can find out what photosynthesis needs: sunlight, chlorophyll and carbon dioxide.

🧮 The method

destarch → light (with a cover) → boil → alcohol → rinse → iodine
  1. Destarch the plant by keeping it in the dark for 2 days, so it uses up its old starch.
  2. Give it light for a few hours with your test set up (a black paper strip, a variegated leaf, or a bag with potassium hydroxide, KOH, which soaks up CO₂).
  3. Boil the leaf in water for about 2 minutes to kill and soften it.
  4. Warm it in alcohol in a hot water bath until it turns pale; the chlorophyll dissolves into the alcohol. Alcohol catches fire easily, so it is never heated over a flame directly.
  5. Rinse it in warm water, spread it on a white dish and add a few drops of dilute iodine.

We remove the green colour first so that the blue-black colour can be seen clearly.

How the model works: a part of the leaf makes starch only if it has light, chlorophyll and carbon dioxide, building up to the full amount in about 4 hours of good sunlight. A plant that was not destarched starts with starch already in all its green parts. Real leaves give patchier colours, and starch can also move slowly through the plant.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Croton and money plant leaves with white or yellow patches are often used for this test in Indian school labs.
  • Potatoes, rice and wheat are full of starch made in leaves and carried to the tubers and grains; a drop of iodine on a cut potato turns blue-black.
  • Grass under a brick or a plank turns pale yellow after a few days: without light it cannot make food or chlorophyll.

🎯 Try this

  1. Keep the default set-up (black paper strip, destarched, 4 h). Press Next step until the iodine is added. Where is the leaf blue-black?
    Show answerEverywhere except the strip that was under the black paper, which stays yellow-brown. That strip got no light, so it made no starch: light is needed for photosynthesis.
  2. Switch off "kept in the dark for 2 days" and repeat the test. What goes wrong, and why do we destarch first?
    Show answerThe whole leaf turns blue-black, even the covered strip, because the starch made on earlier days was still there. Destarching removes the old starch, so any starch we find must have been made during the test.
  3. Choose the variegated leaf and test it. Which parts turn blue-black?
    Show answerOnly the green middle. The white edges had light and carbon dioxide but no chlorophyll, so they could not make starch: chlorophyll is needed.
  4. Choose the two leaves in bags. Which leaf has starch after the test, and what does that show?
    Show answerOnly the control leaf (the bag without KOH). Potassium hydroxide soaked up the carbon dioxide in the other bag, so that leaf could not photosynthesise: carbon dioxide is needed.
  5. Set the time in sunlight to 0 h, then 2 h, then 8 h. How does the colour in the uncovered part change?
    Show answer0 h: no blue-black at all. 2 h: a dark bluish-brown (a little starch). 8 h: deep blue-black. The longer the leaf photosynthesises, the more starch it stores.

📖 Key terms

  • Starch: a large carbohydrate made by joining many glucose units; plants store food as starch.
  • Destarching: keeping a plant in the dark so that it uses up its stored starch.
  • Variegated leaf: a leaf with patches that have no chlorophyll.
  • Control: the part of an experiment kept the same in every way except the one thing being tested.
