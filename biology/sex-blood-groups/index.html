📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Every baby gets half its genes from the mother and half from the father, by chance. This simulation follows two kinds of inheritance in the same babies.

  • Boy or girl: humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. One pair, the sex chromosomes, is XX in females and XY in males. Every egg carries an X. Half the sperm carry X and half carry Y. An X sperm makes a girl (XX); a Y sperm makes a boy (XY). So it is the father's sperm that decides, never the mother.
  • Blood group: the ABO blood group is set by one gene with three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. Each person carries two of them. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are both dominant over i, and when they meet, both show: IᴬIᴮ is group AB. This is called codominance.

🧮 Genotypes and blood groups

IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi → A · IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi → B · IᴬIᴮ → AB · ii → O XX (mother) × XY (father) → ½ XX girls : ½ XY boys Iᴬi × Iᴮi → ¼ AB : ¼ A : ¼ B : ¼ O

Each parent passes one of its two blood group alleles, chance ½ each, and every baby is a fresh draw, so a family can have three girls in a row or four children with four different blood groups.

Simplified: only the ABO gene is shown. The Rh factor (the + or − in “B+”) is a separate gene, and a few rare variants (such as the Bombay blood group, first found in Mumbai) are left out. Real births also have slightly more boys than girls (about 105 : 100 at birth), which this ideal model ignores.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • In many families a mother is blamed for having daughters. Biology says the opposite: her eggs all carry X, and the father's sperm, X or Y, makes the difference. Finding out a baby's sex before birth for selection is a crime in India (PCPNDT Act, 1994).
  • Blood banks match blood groups before a transfusion. Group O (with Rh−) can be given to almost anyone in an emergency, and AB people can receive from all ABO groups.
  • In India, group B and O are the most common, and AB is the rarest, though the exact shares vary from region to region.
  • Blood groups once helped settle doubts in court about who a child's parents were: an AB parent, for example, can never have an O child. Today DNA tests are used instead.

🎯 Try this

  1. Set the number of babies to 4 and press Babies are born several times. Do you always get 2 girls and 2 boys?
    Show answerNo. You often get 3 : 1, and sometimes 4 : 0. Each baby is a separate 50 : 50 chance, like a coin toss. With 400 babies the share of girls settles near ½ on the graph.
  2. Press “A × B (both carry i)”. Which blood groups can the children have?
    Show answerAll four: A, B, AB and O, each ¼. Two parents with groups A and B can even have an O child, if both pass on their hidden i allele.
  3. Press “AB × O”. Can any child have the same blood group as either parent?
    Show answerNo. The AB parent gives Iᴬ or Iᴮ and the O parent gives i, so the children are Iᴬi (A) or Iᴮi (B), half each. Groups AB and O are impossible.
  4. Both parents are group O. Is a group A child possible? Check with “O × O”.
    Show answerNo. Both parents are ii and can pass only i, so every child is ii, group O.
  5. A mother is group A and her child is group O. What can you say about the mother's genotype and the father?
    Show answerThe mother must be Iᴬi, because the child got an i from her. The father must also carry i, so he could be group A (Iᴬi), B (Iᴮi) or O (ii), but not AB.

📖 Key terms

  • Sex chromosomes: the X and Y chromosomes; the other 22 pairs are autosomes.
  • Multiple alleles: a gene with more than two forms in a population (Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i), though each person carries only two.
  • Codominance: both alleles show fully in the hybrid, as Iᴬ and Iᴮ do in group AB.
  • Gamete: an egg or sperm, carrying one chromosome of each pair.
