📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Your heart is two pumps side by side. The right side collects oxygen-poor blood from the body and pumps it to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide. The left side collects this oxygen-rich blood and pumps it hard to the whole body. So blood passes through the heart twice in every round trip: this is double circulation.

Because the two sides are completely separate, oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood never mix. Birds and mammals need this: they keep their bodies warm, which uses a lot of energy and so a lot of oxygen.

🧮 Key numbers

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume

At rest: 72 beats/min × 70 mL ≈ 5 L/min, about all the blood in an adult’s body every minute. When you run, both numbers rise and the heart can pump 15–20 L/min.

One beat ≈ 0.8 s at 72 beats/min

Atria squeeze (≈ 0.1 s) → ventricles squeeze (≈ 0.3 s) → the whole heart relaxes and fills (≈ 0.4 s). “Lub” is the sound of the valves between atria and ventricles shutting; “dub” is the valves at the exits (pulmonary and aortic) shutting.

Simplifications: the picture is a flat diagram, not the real shape: the real heart is one fist-sized organ and the blood vessels branch into millions of capillaries. The dots move about 8 times faster than real blood. Stroke volume is a rough rule (it rises from about 70 to 110 mL with exercise). In the frog and fish views, the heart rate slider only sets the drawing speed.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Pulse: each squeeze of the left ventricle sends a pressure wave along the arteries; you feel it at the wrist. A doctor’s stethoscope picks up the lub-dub of the valves.
  • Blood pressure of about 120/80 mm of mercury means 120 while the ventricles squeeze and 80 while the heart relaxes. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps to the whole body.
  • Fish have a 2-chambered heart and single circulation: blood loses pressure in the gills, so it reaches the body slowly. That is enough for a cold-blooded animal.
  • Frogs and most reptiles have 3 chambers. Some mixing in the single ventricle is fine because they also take in oxygen through their skin and do not keep their bodies warm.

🎯 Try this

  1. Follow one blue dot from the vena cava. Name the chambers and places it passes before it comes back.
    Show answerRight atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (turns red) → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body (turns blue) → vena cava. It passes through the heart twice.
  2. Watch the valves. Which ones are shut when you hear (or see) “LUB”?
    Show answerThe tricuspid and bicuspid valves between the atria and ventricles. They shut as the ventricles start to squeeze, so blood cannot flow back into the atria; the pulmonary and aortic valves open instead.
  3. Set the heart rate to Running (160). How much blood is pumped per minute, and how long does a full round trip take?
    Show answerAbout 17.5 L/min (160 × 110 mL), and the 5 L of blood go round in about 17 s instead of a minute at rest.
  4. Switch to the frog heart. What colour is the blood going to the body, and why?
    Show answerPurple, a mix: oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and oxygen-poor blood from the body meet in the single ventricle.
  5. Switch to the fish. How many times does the blood pass through the heart in one round, and where does it slow down?
    Show answerOnce (single circulation). It slows down after the gills, because pushing through the narrow gill capillaries uses up most of the pressure.

📖 Key terms

  • Atrium (plural atria): thin-walled upper chamber that receives blood.
  • Ventricle: thick-walled lower chamber that pumps blood out.
  • Artery / vein: arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins bring it back. (The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood, the pulmonary vein oxygen-rich.)
  • Valve: a flap that lets blood flow one way only.
