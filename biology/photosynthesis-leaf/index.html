📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Green plants make their own food. Inside the leaf, tiny green bodies called chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which traps the energy of sunlight. Using that energy, the leaf joins carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil to make glucose, a sugar. Oxygen is given out as a by-product. This is photosynthesis ("putting together with light").

Carbon dioxide gets in through tiny pores called stomata, mostly on the underside of the leaf. Water comes up from the roots through the xylem in the veins. The glucose is carried to the rest of the plant through the phloem, and much of it is stored as starch.

🧮 Key formulas

carbon dioxide + water —(sunlight, chlorophyll)→ glucose + oxygen 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

For every 6 molecules of carbon dioxide and 6 of water, the leaf makes 1 molecule of glucose and gives out 6 of oxygen. Watch the counters in the Numbers panel: they always stay in this 6 : 6 : 1 : 6 ratio.

Limiting factor: photosynthesis can only go as fast as the ingredient in shortest supply allows. In dim light, more light speeds it up. In bright sun, the small amount of CO₂ in normal air (0.04 %) usually holds it back, which is why some greenhouses add extra CO₂.

How the picture works: each dot stands for a huge number of real molecules, and the rates are relative (100 % = the fastest this leaf can go). In dry soil the guard cells close the stomata to save water, so less CO₂ can get in; the water used in photosynthesis itself is only a tiny part of what a plant takes up. At night there is no photosynthesis, but the leaf keeps respiring (using oxygen and giving out a little CO₂) all the time; that is not shown here.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Almost every food you eat, from rice and wheat to dal and mangoes, began as glucose made in a leaf.
  • Leaves are broad and flat to catch light, and thin so that gases can reach every cell quickly.
  • The oxygen we breathe was put into the air by plants and algae doing photosynthesis.
  • Cactus stems are green and do the photosynthesis, because the leaves have become spines to save water.

🎯 Try this

  1. Press Start, then follow one grey CO₂ dot from below the leaf. Where does it enter, and where does it go?
    Show answerIt enters through the stoma in the lower skin, moves through the air spaces between the spongy cells and reaches a chloroplast. Later red O₂ dots leave by the same stoma.
  2. Tap the Night preset. What happens to the rate? Which ingredient is missing?
    Show answerThe rate drops to 0 %. Without sunlight there is no energy to join CO₂ and water, so no glucose and no oxygen are made, even though CO₂ and water are still there.
  3. In full sun with normal air, read the limiting factor. Now choose CO₂-rich air. What happens?
    Show answerIn bright sun the limiting factor is carbon dioxide (the rate is about 70 %). With extra CO₂ the rate climbs to about 100 %, and now light becomes the limit. This is why some greenhouses add CO₂.
  4. Switch off "Soil is watered". Watch the stoma and the rate.
    Show answerThe guard cells close the stoma almost completely to stop water escaping, so very little CO₂ gets in and the rate falls to about 21 %. A wilting plant makes much less food.
  5. Switch off chlorophyll (like the white part of a variegated leaf, such as some money plants and crotons). Does photosynthesis happen?
    Show answerNo. The cells get light, CO₂ and water, but without chlorophyll they cannot trap the light energy, so the rate is 0 %. The starch test on a variegated leaf shows the same thing.

📖 Key terms

  • Autotroph: a living thing that makes its own food (green plants); humans and animals are heterotrophs.
  • Chlorophyll: the green pigment that traps light energy.
  • Stomata: tiny pores in the leaf skin, each opened and closed by two guard cells.
  • Xylem and phloem: tubes in the veins; xylem carries water up from the roots, phloem carries food around the plant.
