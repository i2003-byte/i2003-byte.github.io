📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Molecules in a liquid or gas never stop moving. Each one wanders in a random zig-zag, bumped about by the water molecules around it (follow the highlighted molecule to see this). Nothing pushes them in one direction, yet where there are many molecules, more of them happen to wander out than wander in. So there is a net movement from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration. This is diffusion.

When the concentration is the same everywhere, molecules still cross both ways, but equally often, so the numbers stay level: a dynamic equilibrium. A cell membrane is selectively permeable: small molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, water and glucose can get through, but large ones such as starch and proteins cannot. Cells take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide by diffusion.

🧮 Key formulas

time to spread ≈ distance² ÷ (2 × D)

D is the diffusion coefficient. For glucose in water at 25 °C, D ≈ 6.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s. Glucose spreads 10 µm (across a cell) in under 0.1 s, 100 µm in about 7 s, but 1 cm in about 21 hours! Doubling the distance makes it take four times as long. This is why cells are tiny, and why large animals need blood to carry oxygen.

D ∝ temperature (K) ÷ viscosity of water

Warmer water is runnier and its molecules move faster, so diffusion speeds up: about 1.3 times faster at body temperature (37 °C) and 3 times faster at 80 °C than at 25 °C. In a fridge (5 °C) it is about half as fast. Large molecules diffuse more slowly than small ones.

How the model works: the box is 200 µm wide and the clock runs at real speed. Each molecule takes a random walk with the real diffusion coefficient; molecules are drawn far bigger than life and do not bump into one another. Starch is given one tenth of glucose’s D. The membrane is a sheet with six pores that fit glucose but not starch; real membranes are far thinner and have many more, smaller channels.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • The smell of agarbatti or fresh rotis spreads across a room (helped along by air currents).
  • A crystal of potassium permanganate in a beaker of water slowly turns all the water purple, faster in hot water.
  • In the lungs, oxygen diffuses from the air sacs into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses out.
  • In the classic starch–iodine experiment with a cellophane (Visking tubing) bag, glucose leaks out of the bag but starch stays in.

🎯 Try this

  1. Start with the selectively permeable membrane and glucose + starch. Where do the two kinds of molecule end up?
    Show answerGlucose spreads until both sides have about the same number. Starch stays on the left: its molecules are too large for the pores.
  2. Wait until the glucose numbers are level. Are molecules still crossing the membrane? Look at the crossings per second.
    Show answerYes, about the same number cross each way every second. The net movement is zero, but the molecules never stop. This is dynamic equilibrium.
  3. Compare 5 °C with 80 °C. Roughly how long does it take for the right side to reach half the glucose each time?
    Show answerAt 80 °C it is about three times faster than at 25 °C, and about five or six times faster than at 5 °C, because hot water is less viscous and molecules move faster.
  4. Put 150 glucose molecules on the left and 150 on the right. What is the net movement now?
    Show answerNone: the concentrations are already equal, so as many molecules cross each way. Diffusion only produces a net flow when there is a difference in concentration.
  5. Remove the membrane. Is mixing faster or slower than with the membrane? Why?
    Show answerFaster. Without the membrane molecules can cross anywhere, not just through six small pores, so the concentrations even out in a few seconds.

📖 Key terms

  • Concentration: how much of a substance is in a given volume.
  • Concentration gradient: a difference in concentration between two places; diffusion goes “down” the gradient.
  • Brownian motion: the random jiggling of small particles, caused by molecules hitting them.
  • Dynamic equilibrium: movement continues both ways at equal rates, so nothing changes overall.
