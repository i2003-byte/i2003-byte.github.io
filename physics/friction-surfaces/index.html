📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Friction is a force between two surfaces in contact that opposes their sliding past each other. Even surfaces that look smooth have tiny bumps; they catch on each other. Rougher surfaces have bigger bumps and more friction. It takes more force to start a block moving (static friction) than to keep it sliding (sliding friction), because once moving the bumps don't get time to settle into each other.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

largest static friction = μs × m × g sliding friction = μk × m × g

μ (mu) = coefficient of friction, bigger for rougher surfaces · μk is less than μs

  • While the block is still, friction exactly matches your pull, up to its largest value.
  • A heavier block presses harder on the surface, so friction goes up in the same ratio.

Simplification: the μ values are typical values for wood on each surface. Real values change with dust, moisture and wear.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • We slip on a wet, polished floor or on a banana peel because there is very little friction.
  • Shoe soles and tyres have grooves to grip the road; worn-out tyres are dangerous in the monsoon.
  • Pushing a heavy almirah is hardest just at the start; once it moves, it gets a little easier.
  • Kabaddi players and gymnasts rub their hands or feet to get a better grip.

📖 Key terms

  • Static friction: friction on an object that is not yet moving.
  • Sliding friction: friction on an object that is sliding over a surface.
  • Spring balance: a device that measures force by how much a spring stretches.
