📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Molecules inside a liquid are pulled equally in all directions by their neighbours, but molecules at the surface are pulled only sideways and inwards. So the surface behaves like a stretched elastic skin that tries to shrink. This pull along the surface is surface tension.

It is small, but it can lift water up a thin tube, hold a steel needle on water, and make a small soap bubble push its air into a bigger one.

🧮 Key formulas

T = F / L

Surface tension: force per unit length along a line drawn in the surface (N/m). Water ≈ 0.073 N/m at 20 °C, soapy water ≈ 0.03 N/m, mercury ≈ 0.49 N/m.

h = 2T cos θ / (rρg)

Capillary rise (Jurin’s law). The surface pulls up along the rim, 2πr·T cos θ, and holds a column of weight πr²hρg. θ = contact angle, r = tube radius. Half the radius → twice the height. If θ > 90° (mercury on glass, water on wax), cos θ is negative and the level falls.

Δp = 2T/R (drop)  ·  Δp = 4T/R (soap bubble)

Excess pressure inside a curved surface of radius R. A soap bubble has two surfaces (inside and outside of the film), hence 4T/R. A smaller bubble has the higher pressure.

Simplifications: the contact angles (0° for clean glass, 105° for wax, 140° for mercury) and surface tensions are typical values at 20 °C. The tubes are drawn wider than life. For the needle, only the two surface pulls hold it up; the small upthrust of the water pushed aside by the dent is ignored, so real needles can be a little thicker. In the bubbles scene the air is taken as incompressible and the flow is slowed down so you can watch it.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Wicks and towels: oil climbs the narrow gaps in the cotton wick of a diya; a towel soaks up water the same way.
  • Plants draw water up thin xylem vessels partly by capillary action (most of the lift comes from evaporation at the leaves).
  • Pond skaters walk on water: their legs are covered with waxy hairs, so the water surface dips under them like a trampoline instead of wetting them.
  • Soap and detergents lower surface tension so water can spread into fabric and lift away oil and dirt.
  • Farmers plough fields after rain: breaking the fine channels in the top soil stops water from rising by capillarity and evaporating.

🎯 Try this

  1. With water and r = 0.25 mm, read the rise in the three tubes. What is the pattern?
    Show answer5.94 cm, 2.97 cm and 1.49 cm. Doubling the radius halves the height, so h × r stays the same (about 14.9 mm²).
  2. Change the liquid to mercury. What happens to the level and the shape of the surface?
    Show answerThe level falls below the outside level (about 2.2 cm in the thinnest tube at r = 0.25 mm) and the surface bulges upwards (convex), because mercury does not wet glass (θ ≈ 140°).
  3. Float the 1.2 mm needle, then switch on “add a drop of soap”. Why does it sink?
    Show answerSoap lowers T from 0.073 to 0.030 N/m. The greatest upward pull, 2T = 60 mN per metre, is now less than the needle’s weight of 86.5 mN per metre.
  4. Without soap, find the thickest needle that still floats.
    Show answerAbout 1.55 mm. Weight grows as d², but the surface pull 2T stays fixed, so thick needles sink.
  5. In the bubbles scene, start with a 1.5 cm and a 3 cm bubble. Which one grows when the tap opens, and when does the flow stop?
    Show answerThe big one grows. The small bubble has the higher pressure (4T/R = 6.7 Pa vs 3.3 Pa), so it empties until only a shallow cap is left whose curve matches the big bubble (both R ≈ 3.1 cm).

📖 Key terms

  • Contact angle (θ): the angle between the liquid surface and the solid, measured through the liquid. Below 90° the liquid wets the solid.
  • Meniscus: the curved surface of a liquid in a tube: concave for water, convex for mercury.
  • Capillarity: the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube.
