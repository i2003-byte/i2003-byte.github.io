📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Push harder and more charge flows. For a metal wire kept at the same temperature, the current I through it is directly proportional to the potential difference V across it. Double the voltage and the current doubles. This is Ohm's law, found by Georg Simon Ohm in 1827.

The ratio V/I stays the same for that wire. We call it the wire's resistance R, measured in ohms (Ω). So a V–I graph of a metal wire is a straight line through the origin, and a steeper line means a smaller resistance.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

V = I × R

1 Ω is the resistance of a conductor that lets 1 A flow when 1 V is applied

R = ρ × L ÷ A, where A = π d² ÷ 4

ρ (rho) is the resistivity of the material in Ω m. Nichrome 100 × 10⁻⁸, constantan 49 × 10⁻⁸, manganin 44 × 10⁻⁸, copper only 1.62 × 10⁻⁸

  • The ammeter goes in series (all the current passes through it). The voltmeter goes in parallel, across the wire.
  • Longer wire → more resistance (R ∝ L). Thicker wire → less resistance (R ∝ 1/A).
  • Resistance depends on the material and temperature, not on the voltage you apply.

Simplification: the wires are assumed to stay at room temperature. In a real lab, a large current heats the wire and its resistance rises a little, so keep currents small. The torch bulb uses a simple classroom model of a hot filament, not data for a real bulb.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Heater, iron and toaster coils use nichrome: high resistivity, and it does not oxidise (burn) when red hot.
  • Connecting wires and house wiring use copper or aluminium, which have very low resistivity, so they waste little energy.
  • A filament bulb does not obey Ohm's law: its tungsten filament gets about 2000 °C hotter when lit, so its resistance is many times larger than when cold.

🎯 Try this

  1. With 1 m of 0.3 mm nichrome, record readings at 1.5, 3, 4.5 and 6 V. What shape is the graph, and what is R?
    Show answerA straight line through the origin. Every reading gives V/I ≈ 14.1 Ω, and the slope gives the same R.
  2. Double the length to 2 m. What happens to the current at 3 V?
    Show answerIt halves, from about 0.21 A to about 0.11 A, because R doubles to about 28.3 Ω. The line on the graph is less steep.
  3. Go back to 1 m and double the thickness to 0.6 mm. By how much does R change?
    Show answerThe area is π d²/4, so doubling d makes the area 4 times larger. R becomes one quarter: about 3.5 Ω.
  4. Switch to the torch bulb and press “Fill 5 readings”. Is the graph a straight line? Work out V/I at 1 V and at 2.5 V.
    Show answerNo, it bends over. At 1 V, V/I ≈ 1 ÷ 0.173 ≈ 5.8 Ω; at 2.5 V it is 2.5 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 8.3 Ω. The hotter filament has more resistance.
  5. A nichrome wire and a manganin wire have the same length and thickness. Which gives the steeper V–I line?
    Show answerManganin. Its resistivity (44 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m) is less than nichrome's (100 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m), so its resistance is smaller and more current flows for each volt.

📖 Key terms

  • Potential difference (V): the work done to move 1 coulomb of charge between two points, in volts.
  • Current (I): the charge flowing per second, in amperes.
  • Resistance (R): how strongly a conductor opposes the current, in ohms.
  • Resistivity (ρ): the resistance of a 1 m long, 1 m² cross-section piece of a material; it depends only on the material and temperature.
  • Ohmic conductor: one whose V–I graph is a straight line through the origin.
