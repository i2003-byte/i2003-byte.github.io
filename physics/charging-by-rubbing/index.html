📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Everything is made of atoms, and atoms contain tiny negative particles called electrons. When you rub two different materials together, some electrons move from one to the other. The material that gains electrons gets a negative charge; the one that loses them is left with an equal positive charge. No charge is made or destroyed; it only moves. Charges at rest like this are called static electricity.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

q in coulombs (1 nC = 0.000 000 001 C) · r = distance in metres · k = 9 × 10⁹ N m²/C² · one electron carries 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

  • A balloon, comb or polythene gains electrons and becomes negative; a glass rod rubbed with silk loses electrons and becomes positive.
  • A charged object also pulls on uncharged things such as paper bits, by pulling their charges slightly apart (induction).
  • Metals let charge flow through them, so a metal spoon held in your hand does not stay charged.

Simplification: the charge amounts, the leak-away times and the field needed to lift paper (80 kV/m) are simple typical values. The hanging balloon keeps a fixed charge of −80 nC.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • After combing dry hair in winter, the comb picks up small bits of paper and your hair may stand up.
  • You may feel a small shock or hear a crackle when you take off a woollen sweater or touch a metal door handle.
  • On humid monsoon days these effects almost vanish, because moist air lets the charge leak away quickly.
  • Photocopiers and laser printers use static charges to hold toner powder on the paper.

🎯 Try this

  1. Rub the balloon on the hair and bring it near the hanging balloon. Which way does it swing? Now choose the glass rod and silk and try again.
    Show answerThe rubbed balloon is negative like the hanging one, so it pushes it away. The glass rod is positive, so it pulls the negative balloon closer. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  2. After rubbing, look at the charge on the balloon and on the hair. What do you notice?
    Show answerThey are equal in size and opposite in sign. The electrons the balloon gained came from the hair.
  3. Rub the steel spoon with wool. Can it pick up the paper bits?
    Show answerNo. Metal is a conductor, so the charge flows away through your hand at once. The wool is still charged, though.
  4. Charge the balloon, then change the humidity from a dry winter day to a monsoon day. What happens to the graph?
    Show answerOn a dry day half the charge takes about 5 minutes to leak away; on a monsoon day it takes only about 7 seconds, so the charge line drops quickly to zero and the paper bits fall.

📖 Key terms

  • Electric charge: a property of matter; it comes in two kinds, positive and negative.
  • Static electricity: electric charge that stays on an object instead of flowing.
  • Conductor / insulator: a material that lets charge flow easily (metals) / one that does not (plastic, glass, rubber, dry hair).
