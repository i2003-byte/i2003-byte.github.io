📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When water flows into a narrower part of a pipe, the same amount must get through every second, so it has to speed up. To speed water up you must push it from behind harder than from in front, so the pressure in the narrow, fast part is lower. In short: where a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower. This is Bernoulli’s principle, and it is really the law of conservation of energy for a flowing fluid.

The same idea helps explain why an aeroplane wing lifts, why a spray pump pulls up liquid, and why it is dangerous to stand close to a fast train.

🧮 Key formulas

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂

Equation of continuity: the volume flowing per second (Q) is the same everywhere. Area A = πd²/4, so halving the diameter makes the water 4 times faster.

p + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

Bernoulli’s equation along a streamline. p = pressure, ρ = density, v = speed, h = height. In a level pipe the ρgh part is the same everywhere, so p₁ − p₂ = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²).

h = p / (ρg)

An open tube on the pipe fills to a height h that shows the pressure above the air outside. 1 kPa ≈ 10.2 cm of water.

Lift L = ½ρv²·S·C_L

S = wing area, C_L = lift coefficient (about 0.3 + 2π × angle in radians for this wing, before it stalls).

Simplifications: water and air are treated as ideal (no friction, no squashing), so the outlet tube reads the same as the inlet; a real pipe loses a little pressure along its length. The aeroplane is a typical 1000 kg trainer with 16 m² of wing, and the top and bottom air speeds are drawn so that Bernoulli gives the same lift. A wing also lifts because it turns the air downwards (Newton’s third law); both are two views of the same flow. The train’s air speed is a rough model (half the train’s speed at its side, dying away over a couple of metres) and the platform is not drawn to scale.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Venturi meter: water boards and factories measure how fast water flows in a main by the pressure difference between the wide part and a narrow throat.
  • Spray pump and atomiser: fast air across the top of a thin tube lowers its pressure, and the air outside pushes the liquid up into the stream.
  • Railway platforms: announcements tell you to stay behind the yellow line. A train rushing past at 110 km/h drags fast air along its side, and the low pressure there can pull loose paper, a dupatta or even an unsteady person towards it.
  • In a storm, fast wind over a roof lowers the pressure above it, and tin sheets can be lifted off from below.

🎯 Try this

  1. In the pipe, make the narrow part 2.5 cm (half of 5 cm). How many times faster is the water there?
    Show answer4 times: 0.31 m/s becomes 1.22 m/s at 0.6 L/s. Half the diameter means a quarter of the area.
  2. Keep 2.5 cm and an inlet pressure of 4 kPa. Raise the flow rate slowly. When does the middle tube start to suck air in?
    Show answerAt about 1.45 L/s. The throat speed reaches about 2.9 m/s, and ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) becomes bigger than 4 kPa, so the pressure in the throat falls below the air outside.
  3. Choose the wing at a tilt of 6°. Find the lowest air speed at which the plane takes off.
    Show answerAbout 117 km/h (120 km/h is enough). Below that, lift is less than the weight of 9.8 kN.
  4. Set the wing tilt to 0° and then to 14°. How does the take-off speed change?
    Show answerAbout 210 km/h at 0° but only about 85 km/h at 14°. More tilt gives more lift, which is why planes raise their nose to take off. Too much tilt makes a real wing stall.
  5. Choose the train at 110 km/h with you 1 m from its side. Then step back to 2 m.
    Show answerThe pull falls from about 26 N to about 7 N. The fast air, and the low pressure, stay close to the train, so a step or two back makes you much safer.

📖 Key terms

  • Streamline: the path followed by a small bit of fluid in steady flow.
  • Gauge pressure: pressure above the air pressure outside (what the tubes show).
  • Angle of attack: the tilt of a wing against the oncoming air.
