📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Sound bounces off hard surfaces just as light bounces off a mirror, and it follows the same laws of reflection. Draw a line at right angles to the surface (the normal) where the sound strikes it. The angle between the incoming sound and the normal (angle of incidence, i) equals the angle between the reflected sound and the normal (angle of reflection, r), and the incoming sound, the normal and the reflected sound all lie in the same plane.

Sound does not need a shiny surface, but it needs a large one: the surface should be bigger than the wavelength of the sound (about 0.3 m to a few metres for speech). Hard, smooth surfaces such as walls, boards and metal reflect sound well. Soft, porous materials such as curtains, cushions and foam soak most of it up.

We use multiple reflections to send sound where we want it: a megaphone, a horn or a shehnai sends sound forwards instead of letting it spread out; a curved sound board behind a stage sends the sound to the audience; and in a stethoscope the sound of the heartbeat bounces many times along the tube to the doctor's ears.

🧮 Key rules

angle of incidence i = angle of reflection r

Both angles are measured from the normal, not from the surface.

a reflector works well when its size ≫ λ = v ÷ f

For a 1000 Hz sound in air, λ = 344 ÷ 1000 ≈ 0.34 m, so a board 1–2 m wide reflects it well.

How the model works: sound is drawn as rays (the directions in which it travels), which is a fair picture when the surfaces are much bigger than the wavelength; real sound also spreads round edges a little (diffraction), which the model leaves out. Each reflection off a hard surface keeps 95% of the energy and off a soft one 20%. “Sound reaching the listener” is the share of the sent-out sound energy that reaches the ear. Everything is drawn flat (2D), and the pulses move very slowly so you can follow them.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Megaphones, loudhailers used by traffic police, and horns of musical instruments like the shehnai, trumpet and tuba are shaped to send sound in one direction.
  • A doctor's stethoscope: the chest piece picks up the heartbeat, and the sound travels to the ears by bouncing again and again inside the tubes.
  • Concert halls, auditoriums and some temples and mosques have curved ceilings or a curved sound board behind the stage, so sound spreads evenly to the audience.
  • The Gol Gumbaz in Vijayapura (Bijapur), Karnataka has a huge dome: a whisper near the wall of its gallery travels round the dome by reflection and is heard clearly on the far side.

📖 Key terms

  • Normal: a line at right angles to the surface at the point where the sound strikes.
  • Angle of incidence / reflection: the angles of the incoming / reflected sound from the normal.
  • Multiple reflection: sound reflecting again and again from surfaces, used to guide it.
  • Sound board: a curved board behind a speaker that reflects sound towards the audience.
