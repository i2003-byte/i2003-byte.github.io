📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A fixed amount of gas is described by three quantities: its pressure P, volume V and temperature T. Hold one of them fixed and the other two change together in a simple way. These are the gas laws.

  • Boyle's law (T fixed): squeeze the gas into half the volume and its pressure doubles. The molecules have half the room, so they hit each part of the wall twice as often.
  • Charles's law (P fixed): heat the gas and it expands. Faster molecules hit harder, so the piston rises until the pressure is back to the value set by the load.
  • Pressure law (V fixed): heat a sealed, rigid container and its pressure rises in step with the kelvin temperature.

All three are parts of one equation, the ideal gas equation PV = nRT. Extend the Charles's law line backwards (the dashed part) and it reaches zero volume at 0 K = −273 °C, the absolute zero of temperature. That is why gas-law temperatures must be in kelvin.

🧮 Key formulas

PV = nRT T (K) = t (°C) + 273

n = amount of gas in moles, R = 8.314 J/(mol K). Here n = 0.040 mol, chosen so that 1.00 L at 300 K is at 100 kPa (about normal air pressure). Note that kPa × L = J.

Boyle: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ Charles: V₁ ÷ T₁ = V₂ ÷ T₂ Pressure law: P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂

Simplifications: an ideal gas (real gases turn to liquid long before 0 K), a frictionless piston, and changes made slowly enough that the gas stays at the stated temperature. The moving molecules are only an illustration; the numbers come from PV = nRT.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Press the plunger of a closed syringe and it gets harder to push: the air inside reaches a higher pressure (Boyle's law).
  • A balloon left in a hot car grows, and one taken into a freezer shrinks (Charles's law).
  • A pressure cooker or a sealed aerosol can must never be heated empty: the pressure inside rises with temperature (pressure law).
  • Hot-air balloons rise because heated air expands, so the same mass of air takes up more room and becomes less dense.

  1. In Boyle's law mode at 300 K, change the volume from 1 L to 0.5 L and then to 2 L. What happens to P, and to P × V?
    Show answerP goes from 100 kPa to 200 kPa, then to 50 kPa. P × V stays 100 J every time. The points trace a curve (a hyperbola), not a straight line.
  2. Still in Boyle's law mode, press “Show molecules” and compare the hits on the piston per second at 1 L and at 0.5 L.
    Show answerThe hit rate roughly doubles at 0.5 L, just like the pressure. The molecules have half as far to travel between hits.
  3. Switch to Charles's law with a 100 kPa load. Heat the gas from 150 K to 300 K and then to 600 K. How does the volume change?
    Show answer0.5 L, then 1.0 L, then 2.0 L. Doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the volume. Doubling the Celsius temperature does not: 27 °C to 54 °C is only 300 K to 327 K.
  4. In Charles's law mode, raise the load to 200 kPa at 300 K. What volume does the gas settle at?
    Show answer0.5 L. The heavier load needs twice the pressure, so at the same temperature the gas is squeezed to half the volume (Boyle's law again).
  5. In pressure-law mode at 1 L, cool the gas to 150 K and then heat it to 600 K. What are the pressures? Where would the line reach zero?
    Show answer50 kPa at 150 K and 200 kPa at 600 K: four times the kelvin temperature, four times the pressure. The dashed line reaches zero pressure at 0 K (−273 °C).

📖 Key terms

  • Isothermal change: a change at constant temperature (Boyle's law).
  • Absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C, the lowest possible temperature.
  • Mole: 6.02 × 10²³ particles; 0.040 mol of air is about 2.4 × 10²² molecules.
