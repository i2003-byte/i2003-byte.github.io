📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured how strong this force is with a sensitive twisting balance. He found that the force between two small charged bodies:

  • is proportional to the product of the charges, q₁ × q₂;
  • is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, 1 ÷ r²;
  • acts along the line joining them, and is equal and opposite on the two charges (Newton's third law).

It looks just like Newton's law of gravitation, but it is enormously stronger: between a proton and an electron the electric force is about 10³⁹ times the gravitational pull.

🧮 Key formulas

F = k q₁ q₂ ÷ (K r²) k = 1 ÷ (4π ε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²

K is the dielectric constant of the medium (1 for vacuum, almost exactly 1 for air). The same charges in water feel only 1/80 of the force they feel in air. In this simulation F > 0 means repulsion and F < 0 means attraction.

F on q₃ = F₁₃ + F₂₃ (add as vectors)

Principle of superposition: each pair of charges acts as if the others were not there, and the forces are added like vectors (parallelogram law).

Simplifications: the charges are points (tiny spheres), held still, and nothing else is nearby. Glass is given as K ≈ 6; real glasses range from about 4 to 10. Force arrows in the two-charge picture are drawn to scale with full length = the force at 8 cm; closer charges go off that scale.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Salt dissolves easily in water because water (K ≈ 80) weakens the attraction between Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions about 80 times.
  • A comb rubbed in dry hair picks up bits of paper: the charges are tiny (around a nanocoulomb), but the pieces are very light.
  • A microcoulomb is a big charge in everyday life: two 1 μC charges 10 cm apart push with about 0.9 N, about the weight of a small apple (90 g).

🎯 Try this

  1. With q₁ = +2 μC, q₂ = −3 μC and r = 10 cm in air, work out F before you look.
    Show answerF = 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.1² = 5.4 N, attraction. That is about the weight of 0.55 kg.
  2. Drag q₂ from 10 cm to 20 cm. What happens to F? What about 30 cm?
    Show answerAt 20 cm F is one-fourth, 1.35 N. At 30 cm it is one-ninth, 0.6 N. The orange marker on the graph always sits at one-fourth of the blue one.
  3. Double q₁ to +4 μC. Then make both charges negative. What changes?
    Show answerDoubling one charge doubles the force (10.8 N). With two negative charges the size is the same as with two positive charges, but the force becomes repulsion.
  4. Change the medium to water. Why are the forces now so much smaller?
    Show answerWater molecules are polar. They turn so that their ends partly cover each charge, which weakens the field. F becomes 5.4 ÷ 80 ≈ 0.068 N.
  5. Choose three charges and set q₁ = q₂ = +2 μC, q₃ = +1 μC. Drag q₃ onto the line straight above the middle of q₁ and q₂. Which way is the net force?
    Show answerStraight up. F₁₃ and F₂₃ are equal in size, and their sideways parts cancel, leaving only the upward parts.
  6. In three-charge mode with q₁ = +2 μC and q₂ = +2 μC, where can q₃ feel no force at all?
    Show answerExactly midway between q₁ and q₂, where the two pushes are equal and opposite. (With +2 μC and −2 μC there is no such point between them: both forces point the same way.)

📖 Key terms

  • Coulomb (C): the SI unit of charge. 1 μC = 10⁻⁶ C. One electron carries 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Permittivity of free space (ε₀): 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻²; k = 1 ÷ (4π ε₀).
  • Dielectric constant (K): how many times weaker the force is in a medium than in vacuum.
  • Superposition: the total force is the vector sum of the separate forces.
