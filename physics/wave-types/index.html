📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A wave carries energy from one place to another, but the particles of the medium do not travel with it. Each particle only oscillates about its own rest position, a little later than the one before it. Follow the red particle: it never moves along with the wave.

In a transverse wave the particles move at right angles to the direction the wave travels (up and down on a string). The high points are crests and the low points are troughs. In a longitudinal wave the particles move back and forth along the direction the wave travels (a slinky pushed in and out, or air carrying sound). Crowded regions are compressions and spread-out regions are rarefactions.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Wave speed: v = f λ Time period: T = 1 ÷ f Displacement: y = A sin(2πf (t − x ÷ v))

λ (wavelength) is the distance between two neighbouring crests, or two neighbouring compressions. A crest and the next trough, or a compression and the next rarefaction, are λ ÷ 2 apart. The wave moves forward by one wavelength in one time period. The fastest a particle moves is 2πfA, which has nothing to do with the wave speed v.

Simplifications: the row is 2 m long and has no friction, so the amplitude stays the same. The wave speed is set by the medium, so here you choose it directly. The transverse row is drawn to scale unless the canvas is short. Frequency, speed and amplitude are limited so that coils in the longitudinal row never pass through each other.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Sound in air is longitudinal: a loudspeaker cone pushes the air into compressions and rarefactions.
  • A "Mexican wave" in a cricket stadium is transverse: each person only stands up and sits down, but the wave runs round the ground.
  • Earthquakes send out both kinds. Longitudinal P waves arrive first; transverse S waves cannot travel through liquids, which is how we know Earth's outer core is liquid.
  • Light is a transverse wave, but it needs no particles at all: it is made of changing electric and magnetic fields.

📖 Key terms

  • Amplitude (A): the largest displacement of a particle from its rest position.
  • Wavelength (λ): the length of one complete wave.
  • Frequency (f): the number of oscillations per second, in hertz (Hz).
  • Mechanical wave: a wave that needs a medium (string, spring, air, water) to travel.
