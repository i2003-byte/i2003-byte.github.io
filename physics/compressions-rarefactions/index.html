📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Sound is made by vibrating objects. As the cone of a loudspeaker (or the prong of a tuning fork) moves forward, it pushes the air particles in front of it closer together: a region of high pressure called a compression (C). As it moves back, it leaves a region where the particles are spread out: a rarefaction (R), with low pressure. These compressions and rarefactions travel outwards one after another.

Watch the red particle: it only moves back and forth around its own place. The particles do not travel with the sound; only the disturbance (and the energy) moves along. Because the particles vibrate along the direction the wave travels, sound is a longitudinal wave, just like a pulse sent along a stretched slinky by pushing and pulling one end. Sound needs a medium (a solid, liquid or gas) and cannot travel through a vacuum.

🧮 Key formulas

v = f × λ

Wavelength λ is the distance between two neighbouring compressions (or two rarefactions). Frequency f is the number of compressions passing a point each second, in hertz (Hz). v is the speed of sound. In air at 20 °C, a 343 Hz note has λ = 343 ÷ 343 = 1 m.

T = 1 ÷ f

The time period is the time for one complete vibration. A 250 Hz note has T = 0.004 s = 4 ms.

The speed depends on the medium, not on the frequency: about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C (faster in warmer air, about 0.6 m/s more for each 1 °C), 1482 m/s in water and nearly 6000 m/s in steel. So for the same frequency, the wavelength is longer in water and longer still in steel.

How the model works: the motion is shown 300 times slower than real, and the back-and-forth movement is hugely exaggerated so you can see it; in real sound, air particles move less than a thousandth of a millimetre. The picture is 3 m wide for air at 20 °C and is stretched for other media so the waves still fit.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Put your hand in front of a loudspeaker playing a deep bass note: you can feel the air being pushed and pulled.
  • A tabla or dholak skin moves in and out, sending compressions and rarefactions into the air.
  • You see lightning before you hear thunder: light is almost instant, while sound covers about 1 km in 3 seconds.
  • Putting your ear to a railway line lets you hear a train sooner, because sound travels about 17 times faster in steel than in air.

📖 Key terms

  • Longitudinal wave: particles vibrate along the direction in which the wave travels.
  • Compression / rarefaction: regions of higher / lower pressure and density than normal.
  • Amplitude: the largest disturbance from the normal value; a larger amplitude gives a louder sound.
  • Medium: the material (solid, liquid or gas) that carries the sound.
