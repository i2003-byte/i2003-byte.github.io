📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Every electric charge changes the space around it. Put another charge anywhere in that space and it feels a force. We call this region the electric field, and describe it at each point by the force on a tiny +1 C test charge: E = F ÷ q₀.

With many charges, the field at a point is simply the vector sum of the fields of each charge on its own. This is the principle of superposition, and it is all this simulation does.

We picture the field with field lines and the potential with equipotential lines (dashed green): every point on one of them is at the same potential V.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

E = k q ÷ r² V = k q ÷ r k = 1 ÷ (4π ε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²

E is a vector (it points away from + and towards −); V is a plain number with a sign. For many charges, add the E vectors and add the V numbers.

  • Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or go off to infinity).
  • Where lines are crowded, the field is strong.
  • Two field lines never cross: the field has only one direction at a point.
  • Field lines always cross equipotential lines at right angles, and point from high V to low V.
  • The number of lines leaving a charge is proportional to its charge: here 10 lines for every q.

Simplifications: the charges are points, lying in one flat plane, in vacuum (or air). Drawing scale: the short side of the picture is 20 cm. Equipotentials are drawn at ±¼, ½, 1, 2 and 4 times V₀ = k q ÷ 2 cm (and at 0 V when there are both signs).

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A photocopier and a laser printer use charged drums to hold toner powder exactly where the field pulls it.
  • Electrostatic precipitators in thermal power plant chimneys charge smoke particles and pull them out with a strong field.
  • Under a thundercloud the field near the ground can reach several thousand N/C, which is why lightning conductors are pointed and earthed.

🎯 Try this

  1. Choose “One positive charge” with q = 10 nC. Drag the test point to about 5 cm from it (a quarter of the short side). What is E? Now go to 10 cm.
    Show answerE = 9 × 10⁹ × 10 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.05² = 36 kN/C. At 10 cm it is a quarter of that, 9 kN/C: double the distance, one-fourth the field (inverse-square law). V only halves, from 1.8 kV to 0.9 kV.
  2. Choose the dipole and put the test point anywhere on the vertical line exactly midway between the charges. What are V and the direction of E?
    Show answerV = 0 everywhere on that line (the + and − contributions cancel), but E is not zero: it points parallel to the dipole, from the + side towards the − side.
  3. Choose “Two equal + charges”. Where is the field zero?
    Show answerExactly midway between them: the two fields are equal and opposite there. This is a neutral point. Notice no field line passes through it and the lines bend away from each other.
  4. Choose “+2q and −q”. Count the lines leaving +2q and the lines ending on −q.
    Show answer20 lines leave +2q but only 10 can end on −q. The other 10 go off to far away, because the total charge (+q) is positive: from far away the pair looks like a single +q charge.
  5. Switch to “Arrows on a grid” and drag one charge around. Why are the arrows never along a dashed green line?
    Show answerMoving a charge along an equipotential needs no work, so the field can have no part along it. E is always perpendicular to equipotentials.

📖 Key terms

  • Electric field (E): force per unit positive test charge, in N/C (same as V/m).
  • Electric potential (V): work done per unit positive charge to bring it from infinity to that point, in volts.
  • Equipotential surface: a surface on which V is the same everywhere.
  • Superposition: the total field is the vector sum of the fields of all the charges.
  • Nanocoulomb (nC): 10⁻⁹ C, a typical charge on a rubbed balloon or comb.
