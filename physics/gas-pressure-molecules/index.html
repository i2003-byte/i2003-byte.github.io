📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A gas is a huge number of tiny molecules moving fast in all directions. Every time a molecule hits a wall and bounces back, its momentum changes, so it gives the wall a small push. Billions of billions of these pushes every second add up to a steady force on each square metre of wall: the pressure of the gas.

This is the kinetic theory of gases. It explains pressure from the motion of molecules, and it shows that temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. Watch the gauge: the red needle is measured by adding up the wall hits in the box; the dashed needle is the kinetic-theory prediction.

🧮 Key formulas

Push from one hit: Δp = m v − (−m v) = 2 m v P = ⅓ (N ÷ V) m v²_rms (a 3-D box)

N ÷ V is the number of molecules per m³, m the mass of one molecule, v_rms the root-mean-square speed. More molecules, or faster ones, give more and harder hits.

Average KE = ½ m v²_rms = 3⁄2 k T v_rms = √(3RT ÷ M)

k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann constant), R = 8.314 J/(mol K), M = molar mass in kg/mol, T in kelvin. At the same temperature every gas has the same average KE per molecule, so lighter molecules move faster.

How the picture works: the box is flat (2-D), where the same reasoning gives P = ½ (N ÷ A) m v²_rms, and the pressure is shown as a multiple of “100 molecules at 300 K”. Speeds are drawn about a thousand times slower than real (the readout gives the real v_rms). The molecules are hard discs; when they are crowded (200 molecules) they take up some of the box, so the measured pressure comes out a little above the ideal-gas value, just as in a real, squeezed gas.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A bicycle tyre is hard because air molecules inside hit the rubber more often than the molecules outside do.
  • On a hot afternoon the pressure in a car tyre rises: the molecules move faster and hit harder and more often.
  • Helium escapes from a balloon faster than air does, partly because its light molecules move about 2.6 times faster than nitrogen at the same temperature.

🎯 Try this

  1. Release 100 molecules of nitrogen at 300 K and watch the pressure for 10 seconds. Does it stay exactly steady?
    Show answerIt wobbles around 1.0 because hits arrive at random moments. Averaged over time it agrees with kinetic theory. A real gas has so many molecules that the wobble is far too small to notice.
  2. Double the number of molecules from 100 to 200 at the same temperature. What happens to the pressure?
    Show answerIt roughly doubles (about 2, a little more because crowded molecules take up space). Twice as many molecules make twice as many hits per second.
  3. Halve the temperature from 300 K to 150 K. What happens to v_rms and to the pressure?
    Show answerv_rms falls from about 517 m/s to 366 m/s (÷ √2) and the pressure halves: each hit is softer and hits are less frequent.
  4. Switch from nitrogen to helium and then to carbon dioxide at 300 K. Which molecules move fastest? Does the pressure change?
    Show answerHelium is fastest (about 1370 m/s) and CO₂ slowest (about 412 m/s), but the pressure stays the same. Heavy molecules hit less often but harder; at the same T the average KE is the same, so P is the same.
  5. Switch off “Molecules bump into each other”. Does the pressure change?
    Show answerHardly at all. Collisions between molecules swap energy around but do not change the total, so the pressure is the same. They do share out the speeds: with bumps on, the followed molecule keeps changing speed and colour; with bumps off, every molecule keeps its own speed for ever.

📖 Key terms

  • Ideal gas: a gas of tiny molecules with no forces between them except during collisions.
  • rms speed: the square root of the average of the squares of the molecular speeds.
  • Elastic collision: a collision in which no kinetic energy is lost.
