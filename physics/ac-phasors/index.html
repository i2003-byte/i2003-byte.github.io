📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

The electricity in your home is alternating current (AC). The voltage of a socket does not stay at 230 V: it rises, falls, reverses and comes back again 50 times every second. A graph of it against time is a sine wave, made by the generators in power stations as their coils turn in a magnetic field.

A neat way to picture a sine wave is a phasor: an arrow as long as the peak voltage, turning anticlockwise once per cycle. Its height at any moment is the voltage at that moment. Watch the dotted line: the tip of the arrow draws the wave.

If the peak is 325 V, why is the mains called 230 V? Because heating depends on v², and the average of v² over a cycle is exactly half of the peak value of v². The steady (DC) voltage that would heat a heater just as much is the square root of that average: the root-mean-square (rms) value, 325 ÷ √2 ≈ 230 V. Meters and labels always give rms values.

The current depends on what is connected. In a resistor it rises and falls in step with the voltage. In a coil (inductor) a changing current makes a back-voltage that resists the change, so the current reaches its peak a quarter cycle after the voltage: it lags by 90°. A capacitor takes the most current while its voltage is changing fastest, so its current leads by 90°.

🧮 Key formulas

v = Vm sin ωt, ω = 2πf, T = 1/f

At 50 Hz, ω = 314 rad/s and one cycle takes 20 ms.

Vrms = Vm / √2 ≈ 0.707 Vm, Irms = Im / √2

230 V rms → Vm = 230 × 1.414 ≈ 325 V. In the USA, 120 V rms → about 170 V peak.

XL = ωL = 2πfL, XC = 1/(ωC) = 1/(2πfC), Irms = Vrms / X

At 50 Hz, 0.5 H gives XL ≈ 157 Ω and 20 µF gives XC ≈ 159 Ω. Doubling the frequency doubles XL but halves XC.

average power P = Vrms Irms cos φ

φ is the phase difference between current and voltage. For a resistor φ = 0, so P = 230² ÷ 100 ≈ 529 W. For an ideal inductor or capacitor φ = 90° and cos 90° = 0: energy goes in for a quarter cycle and comes back the next, so the average is zero.

Simplified: each part is ideal and connected on its own to the supply (no wire resistance, no losses). The current curve is drawn on its own scale so it can be compared with the voltage. Everything is shown in slow motion; the times on the axis are the real ones.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • India’s mains supply is 230 V, 50 Hz. A mixer or a fan on the mains sees the voltage reach +325 V and −325 V fifty times every second, which is why wires and switches are rated well above 230 V.
  • Tube lights with old chokes flicker at 100 times a second, twice per cycle, each time the current passes through zero.
  • Fans and pumps contain coils, so their current lags the voltage. Electricity boards ask factories to add capacitors to bring current back in step (to improve the “power factor”).
  • The USA and Japan (in parts) use 60 Hz. Equipment made for one frequency can run hot or slow on the other.

📖 Key terms

  • Alternating current (AC): current that changes direction regularly, usually as a sine wave.
  • Phasor: a rotating arrow whose length is the peak value and whose projection gives the value at each instant.
  • Peak value (Vm, Im): the largest value reached in a cycle.
  • rms value: the square root of the mean of the squares; the steady value that gives the same heating.
  • Reactance (X): the opposition of an inductor or capacitor to AC, in ohms. It depends on frequency.
  • Phase difference (φ): how far one wave is ahead of or behind another, as an angle.
