📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A distance–time graph is a picture of a journey. Time goes along the bottom (x-axis) and the distance travelled goes up the side (y-axis). While the bus keeps a steady speed, the graph is a straight line. The steeper the line, the faster the bus. A flat (horizontal) line means time is passing but the distance is not changing: the bus is stopped.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

speed = distance ÷ time

for any straight part of the graph: rise (km) ÷ run (h)

distance in a leg = speed × time = v × 2/60 h

each leg here lasts 2 minutes; 30 km/h for 2 min gives 1 km

average speed = total distance ÷ total time

the stopped time counts too, so a stop lowers the average speed

Simplification: the bus changes speed instantly at the end of each leg. A real bus speeds up and slows down gradually, which makes the corners of the graph rounded.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A city bus stopping at bus stops makes a staircase-like graph.
  • A metro train between two stations moves almost uniformly in the middle of the run.
  • Fitness apps plot your morning walk as a distance–time graph.
  • An auto-rickshaw stuck in a traffic jam gives an almost flat part of the graph.

  5. Can a distance–time graph ever slope downwards?
    Show answerNo. Distance travelled never decreases. (A graph of position could go down if the bus turned back, but that is a different graph.)

📖 Key terms

  • Uniform motion: the same speed all the time; the graph is one straight line.
  • Non-uniform motion: the speed changes; the graph has parts with different steepness.
  • Slope: how steep a line is; on this graph it equals the speed.
