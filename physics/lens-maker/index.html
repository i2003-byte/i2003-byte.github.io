📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A lens is just two curved surfaces of glass. At each surface, light bends by Snell's law. A curved surface bends rays at different heights by different amounts, so it can bring parallel rays together at one point (or spread them out as if from one point).

For one spherical surface between two media, the object distance u, the image distance v, the radius R and the two refractive indices are linked by one formula. Apply it twice, once for each face of a thin lens, and you get the lens maker's formula: the focal length depends on the curvature of both faces and on how much denser the glass is than its surroundings.

That last part surprises many people: a glass lens that converges in air converges less in water, does nothing at all in a liquid of the same refractive index, and even diverges in a denser liquid like carbon disulphide.

🧮 Key formulas

one surface: n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n₂/n₁ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, power P = 1/f (f in metres)

Sign convention: light goes left to right; distances are measured from the surface or lens; R is positive when the centre of curvature is on the right. A biconvex lens has R₁ = +R and R₂ = −R. Example: crown glass (n = 1.5), R = 20 cm, in air: 1/f = 0.5 × (1/20 + 1/20) = 1/20, so f = 20 cm and P = +5 D.

How the model works: every ray is traced exactly with Snell's law at each spherical surface. The formulas above are for near-axis (paraxial) rays and a thin lens, so: rays near the axis (blue) meet where the formula says, while rays near the edge (orange) meet a little closer to the lens. This is spherical aberration. The lens is drawn with a real thickness, which shifts the traced focus by a few per cent from the thin-lens value.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Opticians grind spectacle lenses to a chosen power using the lens maker's formula: the same power can be made as a thin meniscus that sits comfortably in front of the eye.
  • The eye's cornea is one curved surface that does most of the focusing. Under water it hardly works (water and the eye have similar refractive indices), so everything looks blurred; goggles put air back in front of the eye.
  • A glass marble or a water drop on a leaf acts as a tiny lens, and a glass rod with a rounded end focuses light inside it.
  • Good camera lenses combine several lenses to cancel spherical aberration.

🎯 Try this

  1. Start with the biconvex lens (n = 1.50, |R| = 20 cm, in air). Use the formula to find f, then compare with where the blue and orange rays meet.
    Show answerf = 20 cm (P = +5 D). Near-axis rays meet at about 20.6 cm (the lens has real thickness), edge rays at about 18.5 cm: that spread is spherical aberration.
  2. Keep |R| = 20 cm and change the shape to plano-convex, then convex meniscus. How does f change?
    Show answerPlano-convex: 1/f = 0.5 × (1/20 − 0) gives f = 40 cm. Meniscus (R₁ = 20, R₂ = 40): 1/f = 0.5 × (1/20 − 1/40) gives f = 80 cm. Less total curvature means a weaker lens.
  3. Put the biconvex lens in water, then in glycerine (set n = 1.47), then in carbon disulphide. What happens?
    Show answerIn water f = 78.2 cm (much weaker). In glycerine with n = 1.47 the lens disappears: no bending at all, f = ∞. In carbon disulphide (n = 1.63) the convex lens becomes diverging, f ≈ −125 cm.
  4. Choose “One convex surface (glass rod)” and parallel light. Where do the rays meet inside the glass? Then use a point object at 40 cm and at 30 cm.
    Show answerParallel rays meet 60 cm inside the glass: 1.5/v = 0.5/20 gives v = 60 cm. A point at 40 cm is at the first focus, so the rays go on parallel inside the glass (v = ∞). At 30 cm the rays still diverge in the glass and seem to come from a virtual image 180 cm to the left.
  5. Set n = 2.42 (diamond) for the biconvex lens. What happens to the edge rays, and why?
    Show answerThe focal length drops to 7 cm, but the edge rays now meet at about 4 cm while near-axis rays meet at about 7.7 cm. Strong bending at steep angles makes spherical aberration much worse.

📖 Key terms

  • Radius of curvature R: the radius of the sphere of which a lens surface is a part; its centre is the centre of curvature C.
  • Paraxial rays: rays close to the axis and making small angles with it; the simple formulas hold for them.
  • Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n₂/n₁ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) for a thin lens of index n₂ in a medium n₁.
  • Power of a lens: P = 1/f with f in metres, measured in dioptres (D); positive for converging lenses.
  • Spherical aberration: rays through the edge of a spherical lens focus closer than rays near the axis, blurring the image.
