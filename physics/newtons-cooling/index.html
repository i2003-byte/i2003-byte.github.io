📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A hot cup of chai loses heat to the cooler room around it. Newton's law of cooling says the rate of cooling is proportional to the temperature difference between the body and its surroundings. At 90 °C in a 25 °C room the chai cools fast; at 40 °C it cools slowly; it never quite reaches room temperature, it just gets closer and closer.

The number k (per minute) tells how quickly a cup loses heat. It is large when there is a lot of surface for heat to escape through (an open top, a wide cup, a fan blowing) and small when there is a lot of chai to cool (a large heat capacity). A lid stops most of the evaporation from the hot surface, which is one of the biggest losses, and a vacuum flask also stops conduction and convection through its walls.

The plot on the right of the picture shows the law directly: each dot is one reading of the rate of cooling against T − T_room. The dots fall on a straight line through zero, and its slope is k. The first few faint dots sit above the line: in the first minute the cold cup itself is soaking up heat from the chai.

🧮 Key formulas

dT/dt = −k (T − T_room) T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room) e^(−kt) k = (heat lost per second per °C) ÷ (m c)

Example: with k = 0.040 per minute, chai at 85 °C in a 25 °C room is 60 °C hotter than the room. After 10 minutes the difference is 60 × e^(−0.40) ≈ 40 °C, so the chai is at about 65 °C.

ln(T − T_room) = ln(T₀ − T_room) − kt

So a graph of ln(T − T_room) against time is a straight line with slope −k. This is how the law is checked in the school lab with a calorimeter of hot water and a stopwatch.

How the model works: each cup is two parts, the chai and the cup wall. Heat flows from the chai to the wall, from the chai's open surface to the air (evaporation and convection; a lid cuts this to a fifth, a fan raises it), and from the wall to the air. Every flow is proportional to a temperature difference, as in Newton's law. The heat capacities and loss rates are rounded values chosen to match everyday cooling times. Real chai cools a little faster than this at high temperatures, because evaporation and radiation grow faster than the temperature difference; the law is most exact for small differences. 1 second on screen is 1 minute.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Pouring chai from one tumbler to another (as at a tea stall) or into a saucer cools it fast: more surface, more evaporation.
  • A kulhad is easier to hold than a steel tumbler. Clay conducts heat poorly, so it feels less hot when you touch it, even though both outsides are warm.
  • Doctors use Newton's law to estimate the time of death from body temperature, and cooks use it to know how long a cake takes to cool.
  • A vacuum flask (thermos) keeps tea hot for hours: the vacuum stops conduction and convection, and the silvered walls cut radiation.
  • In winter, a cup cools much faster in Shimla than in Chennai because the temperature difference is bigger.

📖 Key terms

  • Rate of cooling: how fast the temperature falls, in °C per minute.
  • Newton's law of cooling: the rate of cooling of a body is proportional to the difference between its temperature and that of its surroundings (for small differences).
  • Cooling constant k: the rate of cooling per degree of temperature difference; it depends on the surface, the container and the mass of the body.
  • Evaporation: the fastest molecules escape from a liquid's surface, taking heat with them.
  • Vacuum flask: a double-walled bottle with a vacuum between the walls, which stops conduction and convection.
