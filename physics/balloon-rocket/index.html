📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Newton's third law of motion: whenever one object pushes on another, the second pushes back on the first with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. The stretched rubber of the balloon squeezes the air and pushes it out of the neck backwards (the action). At the same moment the air pushes the balloon forwards (the reaction). The two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel: the air rushes one way and the balloon moves the other way. Because the forces are equal and act for the same time, the air and the balloon get equal and opposite momentum. In the ideal case (no drag, no friction) the momentum bars stay exactly equal, so the total momentum stays zero, just as it was before release. This is how rockets and jet engines work.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

force on balloon = − force on air (equal size, opposite direction) air thrown out per second ṁ = ρ × (area of neck) × u push (thrust) F = ṁ × u neck 5 mm, u = 30 m/s: ṁ ≈ 2.8 g/s, F ≈ 0.085 N ideal: momentum of balloon forward = momentum of air backward (total stays 0)
  • 4 L of air has a mass of about 4.8 g, so the balloon (5 g of rubber, straw and tape) more than doubles its mass when blown up.
  • With drag and friction switched on, some momentum goes to the room's air and the string, so the balloon ends up with much less than the jet.

Simplification: air leaves at a steady 30 m/s relative to the balloon until it is empty, its density is taken as 1.2 g per litre, the balloon is treated as a sphere for air drag, and the straw rubs on the string with a small fixed force. Real balloons push harder when nearly empty and wobble about. The balloon is drawn bigger than the scale of the string.

🌍 In real life

  • ISRO's rockets (PSLV, GSLV, LVM3) throw hot gas downwards at about 2–4 km/s; the gas pushes the rocket up. No air is needed to push against, which is why rockets work in space.
  • When you jump from a boat to the bank, the boat moves backwards.
  • A gun or a Diwali cracker launcher recoils (kicks back) when it fires.
  • When you swim, you push the water backwards and the water pushes you forwards.
  • A garden hose or a fire hose jerks backwards when water spurts out fast.

  3. With drag on, try 1 L of air with the balloon neck. Does it reach the end of the 5 m string?
    Show answerNo. It runs out of air after about 0.4 s and air drag and friction stop it after about 3.5 m.
  4. Compare the narrow straw and the wide tube with 4 L of air. Which gives the bigger push, and why?
    Show answerThe wide tube. More air leaves every second, so the thrust F = ṁ u is about 5 times bigger than the straw's (7² ÷ 3² ≈ 5.4). But the air runs out sooner.
  5. Why is 6 L not faster than 4 L at the end of the string?
    Show answerThe push is the same, but the extra air makes the balloon heavier and bigger. A heavier balloon speeds up less (F = m a) and a bigger one feels more air drag.

📖 Key terms

  • Action and reaction: the pair of equal and opposite forces two objects exert on each other.
  • Thrust: the forward push on a rocket or jet from the gas it throws backwards.
  • Conservation of momentum: when no outside force acts, the total momentum of a system stays the same.
