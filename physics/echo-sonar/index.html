📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Sound bounces off large, hard surfaces such as a cliff, a tall building or the water at the bottom of a well. The sound that comes back is an echo. It has to travel to the surface and back, so it arrives a little later than the original sound. If you measure that delay and know the speed of sound, you can work out how far away the surface is, without walking there.

Our ears keep the feeling of a sound for about 0.1 s. If the echo comes back sooner than that, it mixes with the original sound and you just hear one longer, “boomy” sound (this is part of reverberation in big halls). To hear a separate, distinct echo, the reflecting surface must be far enough away.

SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging) uses the same idea under the sea. A ship sends a short pulse of ultrasound (frequency above 20 kHz) straight down and times the echo from the sea bed. Repeating this as the ship moves draws a map of the sea floor.

🧮 Key formulas

total distance = 2d = v × t d = v × t ÷ 2

d is the distance to the reflecting surface, t the time between the sound and its echo, v the speed of sound. Don't forget the 2: the sound goes there and back.

least distance = v × 0.1 s ÷ 2

In air at 22 °C (v ≈ 344 m/s) this is 344 × 0.1 ÷ 2 ≈ 17.2 m. The yellow dashed mark in the picture shows it.

v in air ≈ 331 + 0.6 × T (m/s, T in °C)

Sea water: about 1531 m/s. So a ship that hears its echo after 2 s is over water 1531 × 2 ÷ 2 = 1531 m deep.

How the model works: for the cliff and the well, the pulse is shown in slow motion so you can follow it (the round trip always takes about 2.5 s on screen), but the clap and its echo play at the real delay if sound is on. The reflected sound is drawn as if it came from a mirror image of you behind the cliff. For the ship, the pulse moves at its real speed, but the ship glides between pings far faster than a real ship, and the sea bed is made up (real in size: a shallow continental shelf, the deep sea about 3 km down, and an underwater mountain).

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Shout into a deep well or across a valley in the hills and you hear your voice come back. In a small room the walls are too close, so you hear no separate echo.
  • Bats send out ultrasonic squeaks and listen for the echoes to find insects in the dark; dolphins use clicks in the same way. This is called echolocation.
  • Navy ships and fishing boats use SONAR to find the depth of the sea, submarines, shipwrecks and shoals of fish. Doctors use the echoes of ultrasound to make scans of babies before birth.
  • Concert halls and cinema halls cover walls with soft panels and curtains to cut down echoes and reverberation.

📖 Key terms

  • Echo: a reflected sound heard separately from the original sound.
  • Persistence of hearing: the sensation of a sound stays with us for about 0.1 s.
  • Reverberation: many quick reflections that make a sound last longer, as in a big empty hall.
  • Ultrasound: sound above 20,000 Hz, too high for humans to hear.
  • SONAR: SOund Navigation And Ranging: finding distance and direction under water with echoes.
