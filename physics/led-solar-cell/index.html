📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

In a semiconductor, electrons can only have energies in two bands: the valence band (bound electrons) and, higher up, the conduction band (free electrons). The forbidden gap between them is the band gap Eg. A p-n junction can use this gap in two opposite ways.

  • LED (electricity → light): in forward bias, free electrons cross from the n side and fall into holes in the valence band. Each fall releases a parcel of energy about equal to Eg, sent out as one photon of light. A bigger band gap gives more energetic photons: bluer light. It also needs a higher voltage to switch on, because each electron must be given at least Eg of energy.
  • Solar cell (light → electricity): a photon with energy more than Eg lifts an electron into the conduction band, leaving a hole: an electron-hole pair. The electric field at the junction pushes the electron to the n side and the hole to the p side, so the cell acts like a battery. Photons with less energy than Eg (long-wavelength infrared) pass straight through.

Silicon is great for solar cells but useless for LEDs: in silicon the energy of a falling electron mostly turns into heat. LEDs use compound semiconductors such as GaAs, AlGaInP and InGaN instead. Making a bright blue LED (InGaN) was so hard that it won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics; it made white LED lighting possible.

🧮 Key formulas

E = hν = hc/λ → λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / Eg (eV)

Red AlGaInP, Eg = 1.9 eV: λ = 1240/1.9 ≈ 653 nm. Blue InGaN, Eg = 2.7 eV: λ ≈ 459 nm. A white LED is a blue LED coated with a yellow phosphor; blue plus yellow looks white.

LED switches on at about V ≈ Eg / e (red ≈ 2 V, blue ≈ 2.9 V) Solar: P = V × I, efficiency = P / (sunlight × area)

One silicon cell gives about 0.6 V whatever its size; its current grows with the light falling on it (9.5 A for a 156 mm cell in full sun). A rooftop panel joins 60 cells in series: about 37 V with no load, and about 286 W at its best.

Simplified: the LED switch-on law is a simple exponential set to give 20 mA at Eg/e + 0.15 V; real LEDs give a narrow band of colours (about 20–30 nm wide), not one wavelength. The sunlight photons are drawn with an even spread of wavelengths, and the cell is at 25 °C (hot cells give a little less voltage). The cell model is the standard single-diode model.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • LED bulbs give the same light as a 60 W filament bulb for about 9 W. India's UJALA scheme gave out more than 36 crore LED bulbs, saving a great deal of electricity.
  • TV remotes use an infrared LED (about 940 nm). You cannot see it, but most phone cameras can: point the remote at the camera and press a button.
  • Rooftop solar panels in India produce roughly 4 units (kWh) per day for every kilowatt of panels, more in sunny states like Rajasthan and Gujarat.
  • Traffic lights, phone screens, torches and car lamps all use LEDs; calculators, satellites and solar street lights use solar cells.

🎯 Try this

  1. Choose the red LED and lower the supply voltage until it stops glowing. Then do the same for the blue LED. Why does blue need more voltage?
    Show answerRed goes dim near 2 V, blue near 2.8–2.9 V. A blue photon carries more energy (2.7 eV against 1.9 eV), and each electron gets energy e × V from the supply, so V must be at least about Eg/e.
  2. Choose the infrared LED. What wavelength does it give, and why does it look dark?
    Show answer1240/1.42 ≈ 873 nm, beyond red light (about 750 nm), so our eyes cannot see it even though it is shining. Infrared LEDs are used in TV remotes and night-vision cameras.
  3. Switch to the solar cell with sunlight 1000 W/m² and press “Best load for this sunlight”. What power do you get, and what is the efficiency?
    Show answerAbout 286 W at 31.8 V and 9 A, with a load of about 3.6 Ω. The panel receives 1000 × 60 × 0.0243 ≈ 1460 W of sunlight, so the efficiency is about 19.6 %.
  4. Try a load of 0.5 Ω and then 40 Ω. Why is the power small in both cases?
    Show answerAt 0.5 Ω the current is at its biggest (9.5 A) but the voltage is only 4.7 V: about 45 W. At 40 Ω the voltage is high (37 V) but the current is under 1 A: about 34 W. The most power comes in between, at the knee of the I–V curve.
  5. Keep the load at 3.4 Ω and turn the sunlight down to 300 W/m² (a cloudy day). Now press the best-load button. What changed?
    Show answerWith 3.4 Ω the panel gives only about 28 W, but the best load is now about 11 Ω and gives about 80 W. The best load changes with the light, so solar inverters keep adjusting the load all day; this is called maximum power point tracking (MPPT).

📖 Key terms

  • Band gap (Eg): the energy gap between the valence band and the conduction band, measured in electron-volts (eV).
  • Photon: a single parcel of light energy, E = hν.
  • LED: light emitting diode; a forward-biased junction that gives out light.
  • Solar (photovoltaic) cell: a junction that turns light into a voltage and a current.
  • Open-circuit voltage / short-circuit current: a cell's voltage with no load, and its current with the terminals joined.
  • Maximum power point: the load at which a solar panel gives the most power.
