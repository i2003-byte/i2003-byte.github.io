📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A clinical thermometer measures body temperature. Its range is only 35 °C to 42 °C because the human body never goes outside it, and each small division is 0.1 °C so doctors can read it precisely. A kink near the bulb stops the mercury from falling back, so you can read it after taking it out — then you must shake it down. A laboratory thermometer has a range of about −10 °C to 110 °C, no kink, and must be read while its bulb is still in the substance.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Clinical: 35 °C – 42 °C (94 °F – 108 °F), 1 division = 0.1 °C Laboratory: −10 °C – 110 °C, 1 division = 1 °C Normal body temperature: 37 °C = 98.6 °F

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A nurse checks a patient’s temperature with a clinical (or digital) thermometer
  • Students measure the boiling point of water with a lab thermometer
  • Digital thermometers are now used instead of mercury ones, because mercury is poisonous

🎯 Try this

  1. Put the clinical thermometer under the tongue with a fever, then take it out. Does the reading drop?
    Show answerNo — the kink stops the mercury going back, so the reading stays at 39.5 °C until you shake it down.
  2. Do the same with the laboratory thermometer. What happens?
    Show answerThe reading falls towards room temperature as soon as it is taken out. That is why you read it while it is still in the liquid.
  3. What happens if you put a clinical thermometer in boiling water?
    Show answerIt breaks — its range only goes up to 42 °C.
  4. Which thermometer would you use to measure the temperature of ice water?
    Show answerThe laboratory thermometer. 0 °C is below the clinical thermometer’s range.

📖 Key terms

  • Range — The lowest to highest temperature a thermometer can measure.
  • Kink — A narrow bend near the bulb of a clinical thermometer that stops the mercury falling back.
  • Bulb — The end of the thermometer that holds the liquid (mercury or coloured alcohol).
