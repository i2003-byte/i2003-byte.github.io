📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

The mains supply in India is 230 V AC at 50 Hz: the voltage swings positive and negative 50 times a second. Phones, laptops and LED lamps need a small, steady DC voltage instead. Making DC from AC is called rectification, and it uses the fact that a diode lets current through in one direction only.

  • Half-wave: one diode passes the positive halves and blocks the negative halves. The output is a row of bumps, 50 per second, with gaps in between. Half the energy is thrown away.
  • Full-wave, centre-tap: the transformer has a wire from the middle of its secondary coil. Two diodes take turns, so both halves of the AC push current the same way through the load: 100 bumps per second.
  • Bridge: four diodes in a diamond. On each half-cycle two of them conduct (D1 + D3, then D2 + D4). No centre tap is needed, which is why chargers and adapters use bridges.

Bumpy DC is still no good for electronics. A large capacitor across the load charges up near each peak, then supplies the load while the diodes are off. The output becomes nearly flat with a small saw-tooth wobble called ripple. Notice that the diodes then conduct only for short bursts near each peak.

🧮 Key formulas

Vm = √2 × Vrms = 1.414 × 12 V ≈ 17 V No capacitor: Vdc ≈ Vm/π (half-wave), Vdc ≈ 2Vm/π (full-wave)

Subtract the diode drops first: 0.7 V for each diode in the path (one in half-wave and centre-tap, two in a bridge). Half-wave gives about 16.3/π ≈ 5 V; full-wave about 10 V.

Ripple frequency: 50 Hz (half-wave), 100 Hz (full-wave) Ripple ΔV ≈ I(load) / (f × C)

A bigger capacitor, a smaller current or a higher ripple frequency gives less ripple. This formula assumes the capacitor feeds the load for the whole cycle, so it slightly overestimates the ripple the sim shows.

Simplified: each diode is a switch with a 0.7 V drop, the transformer is an ideal 12 V (rms) source with 1 Ω of winding resistance, and time runs 100 times slower than real so you can watch each cycle. Modern phone chargers rectify the mains first and then use a fast switching circuit with a small transformer, but the bridge and capacitor are still there.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Every mobile charger, laptop adapter and LED bulb has a bridge rectifier (often a single four-legged chip) and a smoothing capacitor inside.
  • Inverters and UPS units at home charge their batteries from rectified mains.
  • Electric trains on AC lines and many factory motors use big rectifiers to get DC.
  • The faint hum of some adapters and old radios is at 100 Hz: the ripple frequency of full-wave rectified 50 Hz mains.

    Show answerAbout 5.0 V average, but the ripple is the full 16 V: the output drops to zero for every negative half-cycle. Close to (17 − 0.7)/π ≈ 5.2 V.
  2. Switch to the bridge. Which diodes glow on the positive half, and which on the negative half? What happens to the DC output?
    Show answerD1 and D3 on one half, D2 and D4 on the other. The output now has a bump every half-cycle (100 Hz) and the average roughly doubles, to about 9.4 V (a bit less than the centre-tap's 10 V, because two diodes drop 1.4 V).
  3. With the bridge, add a 470 µF capacitor (load 500 Ω). What happens to the DC level and ripple, and for how long do the diodes conduct now?
    Show answerDC rises to about 15.1 V with only about 0.56 V of ripple. The diodes conduct for only about 14 % of the time, in short bursts near each peak that top the capacitor up.
  4. Keep 470 µF and drop the load to 100 Ω (a hungrier device). What happens to the ripple? Compare with ΔV ≈ I/(fC).
    Show answerThe ripple grows to about 2.3 V, because the capacitor drains faster between peaks. The formula gives 0.14 A / (100 Hz × 470 µF) ≈ 3 V, an overestimate. Raise C to 2200 µF and the ripple falls to about 0.5 V.
  5. Set C = 1000 µF and compare half-wave with the bridge. Why is the bridge ripple about half?
    Show answerHalf-wave: about 0.57 V; bridge: about 0.26 V. In full-wave the capacitor is topped up every 10 ms instead of every 20 ms, so it drains for only half as long.

📖 Key terms

  • Rectifier: a circuit that turns AC into DC using diodes.
  • Half-wave / full-wave: uses one half / both halves of each AC cycle.
  • Centre tap: a connection to the middle of a transformer coil.
  • Bridge rectifier: four diodes arranged so both halves are used without a centre tap.
  • Ripple: the leftover wobble in the DC output.
  • Smoothing (filter) capacitor: a capacitor across the load that stores charge near the peaks and fills the gaps.
