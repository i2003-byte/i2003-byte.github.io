📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When only gravity acts on a falling object, we say it is in free fall. Every object in free fall speeds up by the same amount each second, whatever its mass: this steady acceleration is called the acceleration due to gravity, g. Near the Earth's surface g ≈ 9.8 m/s². A heavy iron ball is pulled harder than a marble, but it also needs more force to speed up, so the two effects cancel and both land together. On another world g is different, because g depends on that world's mass and radius. Your mass (how much matter you have) is the same everywhere, but your weight (the pull of gravity on you, W = m g) changes.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

g = G M ÷ R² (M, R = mass and radius of the planet) v = g t , h = ½ g t² , so t = √(2h ÷ g) and v = √(2 g h) weight W = m g (in newtons; mass m in kg) Earth, h = 20 m: t = √(40 ÷ 9.8) ≈ 2.0 s , v ≈ 19.8 m/s
  • The falling time and the landing speed do not contain the mass m, so the heavy ball and the marble always land together.
  • The Moon's g is about one-sixth of the Earth's, so on the Moon you weigh about one-sixth as much, and a fall takes about 2.5 times as long (√6 ≈ 2.45).
  • Four times the height takes only twice the time, because h grows with t².

Simplification: there is no air at all (as in a vacuum), so there is no air resistance. Each g is worked out from G M ÷ R² with rounded masses and radii; real g also varies slightly with place and height, and with the planet's spin. Jupiter has no solid surface, so its lane uses g at the cloud tops at its equator. The lanes are drawn to the same height scale.

🌍 In real life

  • In 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather together on the Moon. With no air there, they hit the ground at the same moment.
  • On Earth a feather or a sheet of paper falls slowly only because air pushes back on it. Crumple the paper into a ball and it falls almost as fast as a stone.
  • A bathroom scale really measures weight but shows it in kg by dividing by 9.8. On the Moon it would show about one-sixth of your mass, even though your mass has not changed.
  • Chandrayaan-3's lander had to be designed for the Moon's weak gravity, about 1.6 m/s², when it touched down near the Moon's south pole in 2023.

🎯 Try this

  1. Drop from 20 m with a 1 kg ball, then with a 60 kg ball. Does the fall time on the Earth change?
    Show answerNo. It stays about 2.0 s, and the marble still lands together with the ball. Free-fall time does not depend on mass.
  2. On the Earth, how long does a 5 m drop take, and how long does a 20 m drop take?
    Show answerAbout 1.0 s and 2.0 s. Four times the height needs only twice the time.
  3. Set the mass to 60 kg (roughly a person). What does the spring balance show on the Earth and on the Moon?
    Show answerAbout 589 N on the Earth and about 98 N on the Moon, roughly one-sixth. The mass is 60 kg on both.
  4. Look at the v–t graph. What does the slope of each line tell you?
    Show answerThe slope is the acceleration, g. Jupiter's line is steepest and the Moon's is the flattest. Each line is straight because g stays constant during the fall.
  5. The Earth's radius were somehow doubled with its mass unchanged. What would g become?
    Show answerg = G M ÷ R², so doubling R makes g one quarter: about 2.45 m/s².

📖 Key terms

  • Free fall: motion of an object under gravity alone.
  • Acceleration due to gravity (g): the acceleration of a freely falling object; about 9.8 m/s² on the Earth.
  • Mass: the amount of matter in an object, in kg; it is the same everywhere.
  • Weight: the force with which a planet pulls an object, W = m g, in newtons; it changes from place to place.
