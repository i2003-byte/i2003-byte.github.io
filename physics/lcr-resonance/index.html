📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Put a resistor R, a coil L and a capacitor C in a row on an AC supply. The coil opposes fast changes, so its reactance XL = 2πfL grows with frequency. The capacitor lets fast changes through, so its reactance XC = 1/(2πfC) falls with frequency. Their voltages point in opposite directions on the phasor diagram (VL up, VC down), so they cancel each other partly.

At one special frequency the two reactances are equal and cancel completely. Then the circuit behaves as if only R were there: the impedance is smallest, the current is largest, and current and voltage are in step. This is resonance, and the frequency is f₀ = 1/(2π√LC).

Press Sweep to turn the signal generator's knob from 10 Hz to 1000 Hz and watch the current climb to a peak at f₀ and fall again. At resonance the coil and the capacitor each get far more than the 10 V supply, because the two large voltages cancel each other.

A radio uses exactly this. The aerial picks up every station at once, but only the station whose frequency matches f₀ drives a large current. Turning the tuning knob changes C, which moves f₀ along the band.

🧮 Key formulas

XL = 2πfL, XC = 1/(2πfC), Z = √(R² + (XL − XC)²), I = V/Z

With R = 20 Ω, L = 100 mH, C = 10 µF at 50 Hz: XL = 31.4 Ω, XC = 318 Ω, Z ≈ 288 Ω, so I ≈ 0.035 A from 10 V.

tan φ = (XL − XC) / R

φ is how far the current lags the voltage. Below f₀ the capacitor wins (current leads); above f₀ the coil wins (current lags).

f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC))

100 mH and 10 µF: √(0.1 × 0.00001) = 0.001 s, so f₀ = 1/(2π × 0.001) ≈ 159 Hz. At f₀, Z = R and I = V/R = 10/20 = 0.5 A.

Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = XL/R at f₀, bandwidth Δf = R/(2πL) = f₀/Q

Q tells you how sharp the peak is and how many times the supply voltage appears across L (and C) at resonance: Q = 100/20 = 5, so VL = VC = 50 V. Δf is the width of the peak between the two frequencies where the power is half the largest (the current is 1/√2 of the peak).

Simplified: the coil and capacitor are ideal (all losses are in R) and the supply keeps 10 V rms at every frequency. In the radio, the coil is 0.1 µH with 1 Ω of resistance and every station arrives equally strong; real FM sets tune in more than one stage, but each stage works like this.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Every radio and TV tuner, and the Wi-Fi and mobile phone circuits in your pocket, pick one frequency out of many with an LC circuit at resonance.
  • Old radios had a gang capacitor: metal vanes that slide between each other when you turn the knob, changing C and so the station.
  • Metal detectors at airports and in the security frames at malls use a coil in a resonant circuit; nearby metal changes its resonance.
  • Resonance can be dangerous too: in power systems a capacitor and the inductance of the lines can build up voltages far above normal, which engineers must design against.

    Show answerAbout 159 Hz, I = 10/20 = 0.5 A, and VL = VC ≈ 50 V: five times the 10 V supply (Q = 5). The phasors VL and VC are equal and opposite, so V = VR = 10 V.

    Show answerThe peak current doubles to 1 A, the peak becomes narrower (Δf falls from 31.8 Hz to 15.9 Hz) and Q doubles to 10, so VL reaches 100 V. Lower resistance means sharper tuning.

📖 Key terms

  • Impedance (Z): the total opposition of an AC circuit to current, in ohms, combining resistance and reactance.
  • Resonance: the frequency at which XL = XC, so the reactances cancel and the current is largest.
  • Q factor: a number for the sharpness of the resonance; also how many times the supply voltage appears across L or C at resonance.
  • Bandwidth (Δf): the range of frequencies around f₀ in which the power is at least half its peak value.
  • Tuning: changing L or C so that f₀ matches the signal you want.
