📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Friction always turns some of the energy of motion into heat and wears parts away. We can cut it down in two ways. A lubricant (oil, grease or a fine powder such as graphite) puts a thin slippery layer between the surfaces so their bumps do not lock together. Ball bearings go further: they change sliding into rolling, and rolling friction is far smaller than sliding friction.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

friction force = μ × N heat made each second = friction force × rubbing speed

N = weight pressing on the pivot = (5 kg wheel + clay) × 9.8 N/kg · μ is 0.4 for dry metal and about 0.004 for ball bearings

  • More weight on the pivot means more friction, so a heavily loaded wheel stops sooner.
  • Friction does not disappear with ball bearings; it just becomes about 100 times smaller.

Simplification: the μ values are typical classroom values, friction acts at an average radius of 1 cm on the pivot, and a small air drag is added so the wheel always stops. Real wheels also lose energy in other ways.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Bicycle wheels, ceiling fans, mixer-grinders and car wheels all turn on ball bearings.
  • We put a few drops of oil on a squeaky door hinge or sewing machine; the squeak stops and the hinge turns easily.
  • Graphite powder is used in locks, and carrom players sprinkle powder on the board so the striker slides freely.
  • Engines need engine oil. Without it, the parts rub, get very hot and can seize up.

📖 Key terms

  • Lubricant: a substance such as oil, grease or graphite powder that reduces friction between two surfaces.
  • Ball bearing: a ring of hard steel balls between a turning part and a fixed part, so the parts roll instead of rub.
  • Pivot: the point on which something turns.
