📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When two sounds of nearly the same frequency are played together, you hear one tone whose loudness rises and falls regularly: wah… wah… wah. These are beats.

The top panel shows why. Fork B vibrates a little faster than fork A, so B's wave slowly slides past A's. When their crests line up, the waves add up and the sound is loud. Half a beat later, the crests of one line up with the troughs of the other, the waves cancel and the sound is quiet. This happens |f₁ − f₂| times every second.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Beat frequency: f_beat = |f₁ − f₂| Time for one beat: T_beat = 1 ÷ |f₁ − f₂| Together: y = 2a cos(π(f₁ − f₂)t) × sin(π(f₁ + f₂)t)

The sound you hear has the average frequency (f₁ + f₂) ÷ 2, and its loudness follows the outline 2a |cos(π(f₁ − f₂)t)|. That outline reaches a maximum twice in each of its own cycles, so there are |f₁ − f₂| loud moments per second, not half that.

Simplifications: both forks are equally loud and keep sounding at a steady volume. The top window keeps fork A's wave still so you can see B slide past it. With sound turned on, the two tones are played together and you hear real beats.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Musicians tune a tanpura, sitar or guitar string against a reference note: they adjust the string until the beats slow down and disappear.
  • Harmonium and accordion makers sometimes tune two reeds slightly apart on purpose, so the beats give a shimmering sound.
  • In the lab, beats with a fork of known frequency are used to find the frequency of an unknown fork.
  • The pulsing hum near two similar machines, such as two ceiling fans or two engines of an aircraft, is often a beat.

📖 Key terms

  • Superposition: when two waves meet, their displacements add up.
  • In phase: crest meets crest, so the waves reinforce each other.
  • Out of phase: crest meets trough, so the waves cancel.
  • Beat frequency: the number of loud moments per second, |f₁ − f₂|.
