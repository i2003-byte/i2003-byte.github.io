📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite charges, +q and −q, a small distance 2a apart. Its strength and direction are given by the dipole moment p = q × 2a, a vector that points from −q to +q. Water molecules and many other molecules behave like tiny dipoles.

In a uniform field the + end is pushed along E and the − end is pushed the opposite way with exactly the same force. So the net force is zero and the dipole does not move as a whole. But the two forces act along different lines, so they form a couple that turns the dipole until p lines up with E.

🧮 Key formulas

p = q × 2a τ = p E sin θ (τ = p × E) U = −p E cos θ

θ is the angle between p and E. The torque is biggest at θ = 90° and zero at 0° and 180°. The energy is lowest (−pE) at θ = 0° and highest (+pE) at θ = 180°; the work needed to turn the dipole from 0° to 180° is 2pE.

  • θ = 0° (p along E): stable equilibrium. Turn it a little and the torque pulls it back.
  • θ = 180° (p against E): unstable equilibrium. The smallest nudge and it flips right round.
T = 2π √(I ÷ pE) for small swings

Near θ = 0 the dipole swings like a pendulum: the restoring torque pE sin θ ≈ pE θ plays the part of mgL θ.

Simplifications: the rod has no mass and carries a 1 g ball at each end, so I = 2 m a². The field is perfectly uniform (as between two large parallel plates) and the balls' own fields on each other are ignored. Air drag is a simple torque proportional to the turning speed.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A microwave oven makes an electric field that reverses billions of times a second. Water molecules (dipoles) keep trying to turn with it, and this jostling heats the food.
  • In a liquid-crystal display (LCD) on a phone or calculator, a small voltage turns rod-shaped molecules, which changes how light passes through.
  • A charged comb attracts small bits of paper because its field turns the molecules of the paper into lined-up dipoles. In a non-uniform field a dipole feels a net force.

🎯 Try this

  1. With the starting values (q = 20 nC, 2a = 4 cm, E = 50 kN/C), find p and the biggest possible torque.
    Show answerp = 20 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.04 = 8 × 10⁻¹⁰ C·m. The biggest torque, at 90°, is pE = 8 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 5 × 10⁴ = 4 × 10⁻⁵ N·m = 40 μN·m.
  2. Press “Start at 90°” with no air drag. How far does it swing, and where does it move fastest?
    Show answerIt swings from +90° to −90° and back, and moves fastest as it passes θ = 0, where U is lowest (the dot at the bottom of the U curve). Energy changes between potential and kinetic, like a pendulum.
  3. Press “Nudge from 180°”. Why does a dipole pointing exactly against the field not stay there?
    Show answerAt 180° the torque is zero, so it can balance, but U is at its highest. Any tiny tilt gives a torque that turns it further away, so it flips over. This is an unstable equilibrium.
  4. Read the net force on the dipole at different angles. Why is it always 0?
    Show answerThe field is the same at both ends, so the forces +qE and −qE are equal and opposite. A uniform field only turns a dipole; it never pulls it along.
  5. Make E four times bigger. What happens to the small-swing period?
    Show answerT = 2π √(I/pE), so four times the field gives half the period: about 0.89 s becomes 0.44 s.

📖 Key terms

  • Dipole moment (p): q × 2a, in coulomb metre (C·m), pointing from − to +.
  • Couple: two equal and opposite forces along different lines; it turns a body without moving it along.
  • Torque (τ): the turning effect of forces, in newton metre (N·m).
  • Uniform field: a field with the same size and direction everywhere, like the one between two large charged plates.
