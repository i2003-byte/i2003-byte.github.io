📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A metal wire is full of free electrons: copper has about 8.5 × 10²⁸ in every cubic metre. Even with no battery they move very fast in random directions, bumping into the vibrating positive ions. Because the motion is random, there is no overall flow and no current.

Connect a battery and an electric field E sets up along the wire. Between collisions every electron gets a small push opposite to E. After each collision it starts again in a random direction, so on average it gains only a tiny steady velocity: the drift velocity. For 1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire this is only about 0.07 mm per second.

🧮 Key formulas

I = n e A v_d → v_d = I ÷ (n e A)

n = free electrons per m³, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, A = cross-section area in m². Current density J = I ÷ A = n e v_d.

v_d = e E τ ÷ m

τ = relaxation time, the average time between collisions (about 10⁻¹⁴ s); m = mass of an electron. Mobility μ = v_d ÷ E = eτ ÷ m.

E = ρ J and ρ = m ÷ (n e² τ)

This is Ohm's law seen from inside the wire. A hotter wire has more collisions (smaller τ), so a larger field is needed to keep the same current. The drift speed for a given current does not change.

Not to scale: in a real wire the random speed (about 10⁵–10⁶ m/s) is roughly a billion times the drift speed. Here the drift is speeded up a lot so you can see it, but the numbers in the readouts are real. The electrons are also shown far fewer and bigger than they are.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Switch on a fan and it starts at once, although a single electron would take hours to travel 1 m. The field spreads through the wire at nearly the speed of light, and every electron along the wire starts drifting together.
  • House wiring uses thicker wire (2.5 mm² or 4 mm²) for geysers and ACs. More area means a lower current density, less heating and a safer wire.
  • Aluminium has more free electrons per m³ than copper, but more collisions, so it is a poorer conductor. It is used in overhead power lines because it is light and cheap.

🎯 Try this

  1. Press “Switch on” with 1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire. Read the drift speed. How long would one electron take to drift along 1 m of wire?
    Show answerv_d ≈ 0.073 mm/s, so 1 m takes about 13,600 s, nearly 4 hours.
  2. Set the current to 0. Do the electrons stop moving?
    Show answerNo. They still move fast at random, but the trail of the followed electron wanders with no overall direction. No drift means no current.
  3. Keep 1 A and make the wire 4 times thicker (1 mm² to 4 mm²). What happens to the drift speed?
    Show answerIt falls to a quarter (about 0.018 mm/s). The same charge per second is now carried by four times as many electrons.
  4. Heat the copper wire from 20 °C to 200 °C at the same current. What changes and what stays the same?
    Show answerThe drift speed stays the same (it depends only on I, n, e and A). τ gets shorter (more collisions) and the field E needed grows by about 70%, because the resistivity rises.
  5. Switch between copper, aluminium and silver at 1 A in 1 mm². Which has the slowest drift, and why?
    Show answerAluminium (about 0.035 mm/s), because it has the most free electrons per m³ (n ≈ 1.8 × 10²⁹), so each one needs to move less to carry the same current.

📖 Key terms

  • Drift velocity: the small average velocity of free electrons along a wire when a current flows.
  • Relaxation time (τ): the average time between two collisions of a free electron.
  • Current density (J): current per unit cross-section area, in A/m².
