📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A wire carrying electric current behaves like a magnet: it can turn a nearby compass needle. This is the magnetic effect of electric current. Wind the wire into a coil round an iron nail and the effect becomes strong enough to pick up pins. This is an electromagnet. Switch the current off and it stops being a magnet, so the pins drop.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

strength grows with turns × current

more turns of wire, or more current (more cells), make a stronger electromagnet

  • An iron core makes the magnet far stronger; wood or air hardly helps.
  • Like a bar magnet, an electromagnet has a north and a south pole. Reversing the current swaps them.

Simplification: we keep the current at 0.5 A per cell whatever the number of turns. (In a real coil, more turns means a longer wire and slightly less current.) Strength and pin counts are relative numbers from a simple model; the compass treats each end of the magnet as a single point pole.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Cranes in scrap yards lift old cars with huge electromagnets and drop them by switching off.
  • Electric bells, door buzzers and loudspeakers all contain electromagnets.
  • Doctors use small electromagnets to take out tiny iron pieces that fall into an eye.
  • Electric motors in fans, mixers and e-rickshaws spin because of electromagnets.

🎯 Try this

  1. Switch the current OFF and ON. What happens to the pins and the compass?
    Show answerOFF: the pins fall and the compass points north again. ON: the nail becomes a magnet, picks up pins and turns the needle.
  2. Keep 2 cells. Try 20, 50 and 100 turns. How does the number of pins change?
    Show answerMore turns lift more pins (1, then 4, then 8). The strength grows with the number of turns.
  3. Keep 50 turns and change the cells from 1 to 4. What happens?
    Show answerMore cells drive more current, so the magnet gets stronger and lifts more pins.
  4. Swap the iron nail for a wooden pencil. Why do the pins stay on the table?
    Show answerWood is not a magnetic material. Without an iron core the coil is roughly a hundred times weaker in this model.
  5. Swap the battery terminals. What changes, and what stays the same?
    Show answerThe poles swap (the compass needle turns the other way), but the magnet is just as strong and lifts the same pins.

📖 Key terms

  • Electromagnet: a coil of wire, usually with an iron core, that is a magnet only while current flows.
  • Core: the material inside the coil.
  • Turns: the number of times the wire goes round the core.
