📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Every object resists a change in its state of rest or motion. This "laziness" is called inertia, and Newton's first law of motion says it plainly: an object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at a steady speed, unless an unbalanced force acts on it. The coin on the card is at rest. The only sideways force that can move it is friction from the card, and friction can give it only a small acceleration (at most μg, about 3 m/s² for a coin on card). If you flick the card fast, it is pulled out from under the coin in a few thousandths of a second. In that short time friction moves the coin by less than a millimetre, so the coin stays where it was and simply drops into the glass. If you pull slowly, friction has plenty of time to bring the coin up to the card's speed, and the coin rides away with the card.

🌍 In real life

  • When a bus starts suddenly, standing passengers fall backwards: their feet move with the bus, but the upper body tends to stay at rest.
  • When the bus brakes hard, passengers lurch forward because their bodies tend to keep moving. Seat belts provide the force that stops you.
  • Beating a carpet or a durrie with a stick makes the carpet move suddenly; the dust stays behind and falls out.
  • A magician pulls a tablecloth quickly from under plates and glasses. Same trick as the coin and the card, done fast and with smooth cloth.
  • Shaking a wet umbrella or a mango tree branch: the branch moves suddenly, the drops and ripe fruit keep their state and fly off.

    Show answerNo. Five coins have five times the inertia, but they also press five times harder, so friction is five times bigger. The acceleration μg stays the same, so the motion is the same.

📖 Key terms

  • Inertia: the tendency of an object to keep its state of rest or of uniform motion. Mass is the measure of inertia.
  • Newton's first law: an object stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless an unbalanced force acts on it.
  • Coefficient of friction μ: a number for how grippy two surfaces are; friction can be at most μ × weight.
