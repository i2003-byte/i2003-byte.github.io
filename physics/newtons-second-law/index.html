📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Newton's second law of motion tells you how much an object speeds up when an unbalanced force acts on it: the acceleration is bigger when the net force is bigger, and smaller when the mass is bigger. In one line, F = m a. Here a spring balance pulls a cart with a steady force. Friction from the track pulls back, so only the difference, the net force, speeds the cart up. A ticker-timer marks the paper tape every 0.2 s. Because the cart goes a little faster in each interval, the gaps between the dots keep growing by the same amount: that is what uniform acceleration looks like on paper.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

net force F_net = F − f , friction f = μ m g acceleration a = F_net ÷ m (1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s²) from rest: v = a t , s = ½ a t² , momentum p = m v F = 6 N, m = 2 kg, no friction: a = 3 m/s², after 1 s v = 3 m/s and s = 1.5 m
  • Double the force with the same mass: the acceleration doubles.
  • Double the mass with the same force: the acceleration halves.
  • The momentum form of the law: force = rate of change of momentum. With a steady force, p grows by F every second.
  • On the tape, each gap is longer than the one before by a × (0.2 s)².

Simplification: the pull stays exactly the same all the way, the wheels' own turning and air drag are ignored, and friction is a fixed μ m g once the cart is moving (the same value decides whether it can start).

🌍 In real life

  • An empty luggage trolley at a railway station speeds up easily; the same push on a fully loaded one hardly gets it going.
  • A loaded truck needs a much bigger engine force than a scooter to pick up speed at the same rate.
  • A fast bowler's long run-up and a hard push give the ball a big change in momentum; a fielder pulls the hands back while catching so the ball's momentum is removed over a longer time, with a smaller force.
  • Pushing a stalled car: at first nothing happens until your push beats friction, then it starts to roll and keeps speeding up while you push.

🎯 Try this

  1. On the air track, run F = 6 N with a 2 kg cart, then with a 4 kg cart. Compare the two tapes.
    Show answerThe acceleration drops from 3 m/s² to 1.5 m/s². The second tape's dots are closer together, and the cart takes √2 ≈ 1.4 times as long to reach the end.
  2. Keep m = 2 kg and pull with 3 N, then 12 N. How do the accelerations compare?
    Show answer1.5 m/s² and 6 m/s²: four times the force gives four times the acceleration.
  3. Choose the rough floor with a 6 kg cart and pull with 8 N. Why does nothing move?
    Show answerFriction can be as big as μ m g = 0.15 × 6 × 9.8 ≈ 8.8 N. Your 8 N pull is less than that, so the forces balance and the net force is zero. Pull with 12 N and the net force is 3.2 N, so a ≈ 0.53 m/s².
  4. A 1500 kg car speeds up from 0 to 20 m/s in 10 s. What net force does it need?
    Show answera = 20 ÷ 10 = 2 m/s², so F = m a = 1500 × 2 = 3000 N.
  5. Look at the momentum readout on the air track with F = 6 N. How much does it grow every second?
    Show answer6 kg·m/s every second, whatever the mass, because force equals the rate of change of momentum.

📖 Key terms

  • Net (unbalanced) force: what is left when all the forces on an object are added, taking direction into account.
  • Newton (N): the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².
  • Momentum: mass × velocity; a measure of how much motion an object has.
  • Ticker-timer: a device that marks dots on a moving paper tape at equal time intervals.
