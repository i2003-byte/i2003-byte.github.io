📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When light passes through a slit that is only a few wavelengths wide, it does not make a sharp image of the slit. It spreads out (diffraction) and makes a wide bright band in the middle, with much fainter bands on each side.

Huygens' idea explains it: every point of the opening sends out its own little wave. Straight ahead they all arrive in step, so the centre is bright. At an angle where the two edges of the slit differ in path by exactly one wavelength, split the slit into a top half and a bottom half: each point in the top half has a partner in the bottom half that is half a wavelength behind, so they cancel in pairs and the screen is dark.

🧮 Key formulas

dark bands: a sin θ = nλ (n = 1, 2, 3, …) → yₙ = nλD ÷ a width of the central bright band = 2λD ÷ a angle to the first dark band θ = λ ÷ a faint bright bands near a sin θ ≈ (n + ½)λ I = I₀ (sin b ÷ b)², b = π a y ÷ λD

a is the slit width and D the distance to the screen. Example: λ = 600 nm, a = 0.1 mm, D = 1.5 m gives first dark bands at ±9 mm, so the central band is 18 mm wide. The faint bands are only about 4.7 %, 1.6 % and 0.8 % as bright as the centre (their peaks sit slightly closer in than (n + ½)λ).

Simplifications: a far screen and small angles (Fraunhofer diffraction), and an evenly lit slit. The drawing at the left is not to scale, its waves are slowed down and drawn brighter in the directions that get more light; the screen strip at the right is to scale in mm. In “compare” the two slits each have width a and are d = 4a apart.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Look at a tube light through the narrow gap between two fingers held close together: you see faint dark lines inside the gap.
  • A laser beam through a hair or a thin wire makes the same pattern, used to measure the thickness of wires.
  • Diffraction limits how sharp a camera, a telescope or your eye can see: a bigger opening spreads the light less, so it shows finer detail.

  1. With the starting settings, drag P to the first dark band. Where is it, and what is the path difference between the slit edges?
    Show answerAt 9 mm (λD ÷ a = 600 nm × 1.5 m ÷ 0.1 mm). The edge path difference is a y ÷ D = 600 nm, exactly one wavelength.
  2. Make the slit narrower, from 0.1 mm to 0.05 mm. Does the central band get wider or narrower?
    Show answerWider: it doubles from 18 mm to 36 mm. The narrower the opening, the more the light spreads, which is the opposite of what you might expect from shadows.
  3. Turn on “Show faint bands 10× brighter” and move P to the first faint bright band. How bright is it compared with the centre?
    Show answerAbout 4.7 %, near 1.43λ of edge path difference (close to 1.5λ). Here the slit splits into three parts: two cancel, one third is left over, so the band is faint.
  4. Switch on “Compare with two such slits”. How do the two patterns fit together?
    Show answerThe two-slit pattern has many narrow, equally spaced fringes of width λD ÷ d, but their brightness follows the single-slit curve: the single-slit pattern is the envelope. Inside the central band there are 7 fringes here, because d = 4a.
  5. Use white light. What colour are the edges of the central band?
    Show answerThe centre is white, but the edges are reddish on the outside: red light (long λ) spreads the most, violet the least.

📖 Key terms

  • Diffraction: the spreading of a wave around an obstacle or through an opening.
  • Central maximum: the wide bright band straight ahead of the slit.
  • Secondary maxima: the much fainter bright bands beside it.
  • Interference vs diffraction: both are waves adding up. We say interference for a few sources (two slits) and diffraction for the many wavelets from one opening.
