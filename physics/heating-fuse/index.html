📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When electric current flows through a wire, the wire gets hot. This is the heating effect of electric current. Heaters, irons, kettles and filament bulbs use it on purpose: their coils are made of a special wire (such as nichrome) that gets very hot.

The same effect keeps us safe. A fuse is a short piece of thin wire that melts at a low temperature. If too much current flows, the fuse wire melts first, breaks the circuit and stops the current before the house wiring overheats and catches fire.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

current (A) = power (W) ÷ voltage (V)

a 2000 W heater on 220 V draws 2000 ÷ 220 ≈ 9.1 A

  • Appliances at home are connected side by side (in parallel), so their currents add up in the main wire and fuse.
  • Overloading: too many appliances on one circuit, so the current is more than the fuse rating.
  • Short circuit: the live and neutral wires touch, the current becomes huge and the fuse melts at once.

Simplification: the temperatures come from a simple classroom model, not real fuse data. Real fuses carry their rated current safely and melt when it is exceeded, faster for bigger overloads, just as shown here. Many homes now use an MCB (miniature circuit breaker), a switch that turns itself off in the same situations and can be reset.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Electric irons, geysers, toasters and room heaters all use the heating effect.
  • The glowing filament of an old bulb is hot enough to give light; most of the energy is lost as heat.
  • Plugging a heater, a kettle and an iron into one extension board can overload it, which is a common cause of house fires.
  • Always use a fuse wire of the right rating. Never replace it with a thick copper wire.

    Show answerThe copper wire does not melt, so nothing stops the current. The house wiring overheats and can catch fire.

📖 Key terms

  • Heating effect: a wire gets hot when current flows through it.
  • Fuse: a safety device with a thin wire that melts when the current is too large.
  • Rating: the largest current a fuse carries safely, for example 10 A.
  • Overload / short circuit: the two faults that make the current too large.
