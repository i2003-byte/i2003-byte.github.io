📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

White sunlight is a mixture of colours. Glass slows violet light a little more than red light, so its refractive index is a little bigger for violet. When white light passes through a prism, each colour bends by a slightly different angle and they fan out into a spectrum: VIBGYOR, with violet bent the most and red the least. This splitting is dispersion. Turn on the second, upside-down prism and the colours bend back and mix into white again, as Isaac Newton showed. Switch to the sky mode to see a different effect, scattering: tiny air molecules throw blue light around far more than red, which is why the sky is blue and the setting Sun is red.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Snell's law at each face: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ Deviation δ = i + e − A Crown glass: n(red) ≈ 1.513, n(violet) ≈ 1.530 Rayleigh scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴
  • The angle of the prism A is the angle between its two refracting faces (60° here).
  • Angle of deviation δ: the angle between the incoming ray and the outgoing ray. It is larger for violet than for red.
  • Flint glass is more dispersive than crown glass; water is less.
  • Blue light (450 nm) is scattered about (650 ÷ 450)⁴ ≈ 4.4 times more than red light (650 nm).
  • Particles much bigger than the wavelength (water drops in clouds, fog, dust) scatter all colours about equally, so they look white or grey.

Simplification: the rays are traced exactly with Snell's law, but the "exaggerate" switch multiplies the difference in refractive index by 4 because the real spread is only a degree or two. The sky colours use just three wavelengths and ignore dust and the way our eyes see colour.

🌍 In real life

  • Rainbow: raindrops act like tiny prisms. Each drop refracts, reflects inside and refracts sunlight again; you see a rainbow with the Sun behind you.
  • Blue sky, red sunrise and sunset: at noon sunlight crosses little air; at sunset it crosses about 38 times more, losing most of its blue on the way.
  • Danger signals are red: red light is scattered least by fog and smoke, so it can be seen from far away.
  • Astronauts see a black sky even in daytime: there is no air to scatter light.
  • Stars twinkle because of a related effect: atmospheric refraction by moving layers of warm and cool air.

🎯 Try this

  1. Read the deviation of red and violet. Which colour bends most, and why?
    Show answerViolet. Glass has a higher refractive index for violet light, so violet light bends more at each face.
  2. Change crown glass to flint glass. What happens to the spread?
    Show answerIt grows: flint glass is more dispersive (its refractive index changes more with colour).
  3. Turn on the second prism. What reaches the screen, and what does it prove?
    Show answerWhite light. The colours were already in the sunlight; the first prism only separated them and the second one recombines them.
  4. In sky mode, move the Sun from 75° to 1°. Why does the Sun turn orange-red?
    Show answerNear the horizon sunlight passes through much more air, and the blue part is scattered out of the beam on the way, so mostly red and orange reach you.
  5. Choose "No air". Why is the sky black?
    Show answerWith nothing to scatter it, sunlight only travels straight from the Sun; no light comes to you from other parts of the sky.

📖 Key terms

  • Dispersion: splitting of white light into its colours.
  • Spectrum: the band of colours VIBGYOR.
  • Angle of deviation: how much the prism turns a ray.
  • Scattering: light sent off in all directions by small particles.
  • Tyndall effect: scattering that makes a beam of light visible in smoke, mist or a dusty room.
