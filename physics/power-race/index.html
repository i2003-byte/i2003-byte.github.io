📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Lifting a mass m through a height h always needs the same work, W = m g h, whoever or whatever does it. What differs is how fast the work is done. Power is the rate of doing work: the work done each second. Here a walker, a runner and a motor lift all raise the same mass to the same floor. They do equal work, but the one who finishes first has the greatest power. On the work–time graph each lifter gives a straight line, and the steeper line means more power. Electricity companies sell energy in a bigger unit, the kilowatt hour (kWh), which is the "unit" printed on your electricity bill.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

P = W ÷ t 1 watt (W) = 1 joule per second 1 kW = 1000 W 1 horsepower (hp) ≈ 746 W 1 kWh = 1 kW × 1 h = 1000 × 3600 J = 3.6 × 10⁶ J = 1 unit 50 kg up 9 m: W = 50 × 9.8 × 9 = 4410 J; in 30 s, P = 147 W
  • Energy used = power × time. A 1 kW motor running 1 hour a day for 30 days uses 30 kWh.
  • Bill = units (kWh) × rate in ₹ per unit.
  • Average power = total work ÷ total time.

Simplifications: each lifter moves up at a steady speed, the motor wastes no energy (real motors are perhaps 70–90% efficient), and only the work of lifting the mass is counted, not the extra energy people use to move their arms and legs.

🌍 In real life

  • Ratings printed on appliances: a ceiling fan about 50–75 W, an LED bulb 9 W, an electric iron 1000 W, a geyser 2000 W.
  • Water pumps that fill rooftop tanks are rated in horsepower, often 0.5 hp or 1 hp.
  • A goods lift in a building and a porter on the stairs can carry the same sack up; the lift does it faster because it has more power.
  • Engines of cars and tractors are compared by power in kW or hp.

📖 Key terms

  • Power: the rate of doing work, or of using energy.
  • Watt (W): the power when 1 joule of work is done each second.
  • Kilowatt hour (kWh): the energy used by a 1 kW device in 1 hour; the commercial unit of electrical energy.
  • Horsepower (hp): an older unit of power, about 746 W.
