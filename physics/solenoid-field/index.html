📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Bend a current-carrying wire into a circle and the field circles from every bit of the wire all point the same way through the middle. So the field inside a loop is much stronger than round a straight wire. Wind many turns one after another along a tube and you get a solenoid. Inside it the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced: the field is uniform. Outside, the solenoid behaves just like a bar magnet with a north and a south end.

The picture is a cut-away view: imagine slicing the coil along its axis. Each turn then shows up as two dots, where the wire passes through the slice going one way at the top (⊙ out of the screen) and the other way at the bottom (⊗ into the screen).

🧮 Key formulas & rules

  • Right-hand thumb rule: thumb along the current in any small piece of wire, fingers curl along the field round it. Inside the loop, all these curls point the same way.
  • Which end is north? Look at one end of the coil. If the current flows anticlockwise, that end is a north pole; if clockwise, it is a south pole. (Or curl the fingers of your right hand along the current: your thumb points to the N end.)
  • The field gets stronger with more turns and more current.
Centre of a loop of N turns: B = μ₀ N I ÷ (2r) Inside a long solenoid: B = μ₀ n I, where n = N ÷ length

μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m/A. These formulas are Class 12; in Class 10 just remember that B grows with N and I. A smaller loop gives a stronger field at its centre. For a solenoid, only the turns per metre matter, not the radius, as long as it is long and thin.

The simulation computes the exact field of every turn (each turn is treated as a perfect circle, and the coil is in air with no iron core). That is why the centre value is a little less than μ₀nI for a short, fat solenoid: the formula assumes an endlessly long one.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Put a soft iron rod inside a solenoid and you have an electromagnet: used in electric bells, cranes in scrap yards and relays.
  • The uniform field inside a huge solenoid is used in hospital MRI scanners.
  • Loudspeakers, door locks and car starters all use small coils of wire.

🎯 Try this

  1. Drag the compass through the inside of the solenoid, then round the outside. How does the field inside compare with the field near the ends?
    Show answerInside, the compass keeps pointing the same way and the field hardly changes: it is uniform. Outside, the lines spread out like those of a bar magnet, and the field is much weaker.
  2. Choose the circular loop with 10 turns, 2 A and r = 5 cm. What is B at the centre?
    Show answerB = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 10 × 2 ÷ (2 × 0.05) ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ T = 251 μT.
  3. Set the solenoid to 30 turns, 10 cm long, 2 A. Predict B with μ₀nI, then compare with the simulation for r = 1.5 cm and r = 6 cm.
    Show answern = 30 ÷ 0.10 = 300 turns per metre, so μ₀nI ≈ 754 μT. A thin coil (r = 1.5 cm) gives about 720 μT, close to this. A fat coil (r = 6 cm) is not “long” compared with its width, so its centre field is only about 480 μT.
  4. Reverse the current. What happens to the poles and the compass?
    Show answerThe N and S ends swap, every field line reverses, and the compass needle turns round.

📖 Key terms

  • Solenoid: a coil of many circular turns of insulated wire wound close together on a tube.
  • Uniform field: a field with the same strength and direction everywhere in a region; its lines are parallel and evenly spaced.
  • Electromagnet: a solenoid with a soft iron core; it is a magnet only while current flows.
