📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

In a healthy eye the image of anything from about 25 cm to far away falls exactly on the retina. Two common refractive defects spoil this. In myopia (short-sightedness) the eye bends light too much, or the eyeball is too long, so light from far objects meets in front of the retina: far things look blurred but near things are fine. In hypermetropia (long-sightedness) the eye bends light too little, or the eyeball is too short, so light from near objects would meet behind the retina: near things look blurred. Spectacles fix both by adding or taking away a little power before the light enters the eye.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Myopia: concave lens with f = −(far point) e.g. far point 1 m → f = −100 cm, P = −1 D Hypermetropia: convex lens with 1/f = 1/25 − 1/(near point) e.g. near point 1 m → f = +33 cm, P = +3 D
  • The concave lens makes light from a very far object spread out as if it came from the eye's far point, which the eye can see.
  • The convex lens makes light from a book at 25 cm look as if it came from the eye's near point.
  • With u = −25 cm and v = −(near point), the lens formula 1/v − 1/u = 1/f gives the rule above.
  • Presbyopia (old age) can bring both problems at once; bifocal lenses have a concave upper part for far and a convex lower part for reading.

Simplification: the eye is a "reduced eye" (one lens, retina 2.3 cm behind it, 4 D of accommodation), the glasses are treated as touching the eye, distances outside the eye use a logarithmic scale, and the spread of rays at the retina is drawn 3 times bigger so you can see it. Real prescriptions are found by an eye test.

🌍 In real life

  • Many students in India find the blackboard blurred: often the first sign of myopia. An eye check at school catches it.
  • A prescription like "−2.00 D" means a concave lens of focal length 50 cm; "+1.50 D" is a convex lens of 67 cm.
  • Contact lenses and laser surgery (which reshapes the cornea) correct the same defects.
  • Cataract makes the lens cloudy; it is treated by replacing the lens with an artificial one, not by spectacles.

🎯 Try this

  1. Myopia, far point 1 m, object at 6 m. Where do the rays meet? Now switch on the glasses.
    Show answerWithout glasses they meet in front of the retina (blurred). The −1 D concave lens makes the light appear to come from 1 m away, so the eye focuses it on the retina.
  2. A student can see clearly only up to 80 cm. What lens do they need?
    Show answerConcave, f = −80 cm, P = 100 ÷ (−80) = −1.25 D.
  3. Hypermetropia, near point 1 m. Can this person read a book at 25 cm? What about with glasses?
    Show answerWithout glasses the rays would meet behind the retina (blurred). With the +3 D convex lens, the book's image is formed at 1 m, which they can see clearly.
  4. A person's near point is 50 cm. Find the power of their reading glasses.
    Show answer1/f = 1/25 − 1/50 = 1/50, so f = +50 cm and P = +2 D.

📖 Key terms

  • Myopia: near-sightedness; far point closer than infinity; corrected by a concave lens.
  • Hypermetropia: far-sightedness; near point farther than 25 cm; corrected by a convex lens.
  • Far point / near point: the farthest and nearest points the eye can see clearly.
  • Dioptre (D): unit of lens power, 1/f with f in metres.
