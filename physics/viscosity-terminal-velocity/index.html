📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Liquids and gases resist being sheared, a bit like friction between their layers. This is viscosity. Honey and glycerine are very viscous; water and air much less so.

Drop a ball into a liquid. At first it speeds up, but the faster it goes, the bigger the viscous drag on it. Soon drag + upthrust exactly balance the weight. The net force is zero, so the ball stops speeding up and falls at a steady terminal speed. Raindrops do the same in air, which is why rain does not hit us like bullets.

🧮 Key formulas

F = 6πηrv

Stokes’ law: drag on a small sphere of radius r moving slowly at speed v through a fluid of viscosity η (unit: pascal-second, Pa·s).

v_t = 2r²(σ − ρ)g / (9η)

Terminal speed from weight = upthrust + drag. σ = density of the ball, ρ = density of the fluid. Double the radius → 4 times the terminal speed (v_t ∝ r²).

Re = ρv(2r) / η

Reynolds number. Stokes’ law is good only when Re is below about 1 (slow, smooth flow). At larger Re the fluid swirls into eddies behind the ball and drag grows faster, roughly as v².

Simplifications: the jar is taken to be much wider than the ball (real walls slow it a little). For larger Re the drag uses a standard formula fitted to experiments (Schiller–Naumann, then a constant drag coefficient of 0.44). Viscosity falls with temperature by a rounded rate for each liquid; honey varies a lot from one sample to the next. Big raindrops really flatten like a bun and fall a little slower than the sim shows (about 9 m/s). The balls and drops are drawn much bigger than life.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Clouds float: a cloud droplet (0.01 mm) has a terminal speed of only about 1 cm/s, so rising air easily holds it up. Only when droplets join into 1 mm raindrops do they fall at about 7 m/s.
  • Engine oil is graded by viscosity. Thick oil protects a hot engine; in winter at hill stations a thinner grade is used so the engine turns over easily.
  • Warm honey or ghee pours much more easily than cold, because viscosity falls quickly as a liquid warms up.
  • Doctors measure the ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate): how fast red blood cells settle in a thin tube of blood, a sign of infection or inflammation.
  • A skydiver reaches about 200 km/h; opening the parachute makes drag much bigger, so the terminal speed falls to a safe 20 km/h or so.

🎯 Try this

  1. In glycerine, compare the ball r = 1.5 mm with the ball 2r in the next jar. How many times faster is the bigger ball?
    Show answerAbout 3.8 times (2.2 cm/s vs 8.4 cm/s). Stokes’ law says 4 times, because v_t ∝ r²; the big ball is just starting to make the flow less smooth.
  2. Switch to water with a 2 mm steel ball. What does Stokes’ law predict, and what really happens?
    Show answerStokes’ law predicts about 59 m/s, but the ball really falls at about 0.9 m/s. Re is in the thousands, so the flow is turbulent and Stokes’ law does not apply.
  3. In glycerine, warm the liquid from 20 °C to 30 °C. What happens to the viscosity and the terminal speed?
    Show answerη falls from about 1.4 to 0.63 Pa·s, and the 1.5 mm ball falls more than twice as fast (2.2 → 4.8 cm/s).
  4. Choose a plastic ball in honey. Why doesn’t it sink?
    Show answerPlastic (1150 kg/m³) is less dense than honey (1420 kg/m³), so the upthrust is bigger than the weight and it floats.
  5. In the sky scene, drop a raindrop and then a cloud droplet. How long does each take to fall 2 km, and how fast would a raindrop hit with no air?
    Show answerThe raindrop takes about 5 minutes at 7 m/s; the cloud droplet would take about 46 hours. With no air it would hit the ground at about 200 m/s (over 700 km/h) after 20 s.

📖 Key terms

  • Viscosity (η): a fluid’s resistance to flow; SI unit Pa·s (water ≈ 0.001, glycerine ≈ 1.4).
  • Terminal speed: the steady speed at which drag and upthrust balance the weight.
  • Laminar flow: smooth flow in layers; turbulent flow is full of swirling eddies.
