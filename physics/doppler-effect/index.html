📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When an ambulance races past, its siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and suddenly lower as it moves away. The siren itself does not change. This is the Doppler effect: the frequency you hear depends on how the source (and you) move.

Each circle is a wavefront that left the vehicle at an earlier moment, centred where the vehicle was at that time. Because the vehicle keeps moving forward, the circles in front are crowded together (shorter wavelength, higher frequency) and those behind are spread out (longer wavelength, lower frequency). The sound still travels at 340 m/s; only the spacing changes.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Source coming towards you: f′ = f × v ÷ (v − vs) Source going away: f′ = f × v ÷ (v + vs) Listener to one side: f′ = f ÷ (1 − (vs ÷ v) cos θ)

v is the speed of sound (340 m/s here) and vs is the speed of the source. θ is the angle between the vehicle's direction and the line from where the vehicle was when it sent the sound to where you stand. If the listener moves too, the general result is f′ = f (v + vₒ) ÷ (v − vs), counting speeds towards the other as positive.

Simplifications: still air at 340 m/s, a vehicle at constant speed, and a listener at rest. Only one circle every 0.1 s is drawn; the real siren sends out hundreds of crests in that time. The graph uses the sound that is reaching you at each moment, so it changes a little after the vehicle passes, not exactly as it passes. The formulas work only while vs is less than v.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • The horn of a passing train or the siren of an ambulance drops in pitch as it goes by.
  • Traffic police speed guns send out radio waves and measure the change in frequency of the reflected waves.
  • Doppler weather radars, used by the India Meteorological Department, measure how fast rain is moving inside a cyclone.
  • Doctors use Doppler ultrasound to measure the speed of blood flow, and astronomers use the shift of starlight to tell whether stars and galaxies are moving towards or away from us.

📖 Key terms

  • Doppler effect: the change in observed frequency due to motion of the source or the observer.
  • Apparent frequency (f′): the frequency the listener actually hears.
  • Wavefront: a surface on which all points are at the same stage of vibration (for example, all at a crest).
  • Shock wave: the pile-up of waves when a source moves faster than the waves themselves.
