📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A plucked string vibrates and makes the air around it vibrate. Three things decide its pitch: its length (pressing a fret makes the vibrating part shorter → higher), its tightness (turning the peg to tighten it → higher) and its thickness (a thicker, heavier string → lower). That is why a guitar has thin and thick strings, and why musicians tune by tightening the pegs.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

f = (1 / 2L) × √(T / μ)

L = length, T = tension, μ = mass per metre

Half the length → double the frequency (one octave up) Four times the tension → double the frequency

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Guitar, sitar, veena and violin players press strings to change notes
  • Tuning pegs tighten or loosen strings
  • The thickest string on a guitar plays the lowest note

🎯 Try this

  1. Halve the string length (0.65 m → about 0.33 m). What happens to the frequency?
    Show answerIt doubles — the note goes up one octave.
  2. Keep everything else fixed and switch from thin to very thick. Higher or lower?
    Show answerLower.
  3. Can you make a thick string play the same note as a thin one?
    Show answerYes — tighten it more, or shorten it.

📖 Key terms

  • Tension — How tightly the string is stretched.
  • Fret — A metal strip on the neck; pressing behind it shortens the vibrating string.
  • Octave — A doubling of frequency.
