📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Burning is a chemical reaction called combustion: a fuel combines with oxygen from the air and gives out heat and often light. A fire needs three things at the same time, drawn as the three sides of the fire triangle:

  • Fuel: something that can burn (paper, wood, kerosene, LPG, plastic).
  • Oxygen: normal air has about 21% oxygen. Most flames die when the oxygen near them falls to about 15%.
  • Heat: the fuel must be heated to its ignition temperature, the lowest temperature at which it catches fire.

Take away any one side and the fire goes out. That is the whole secret of fire fighting: cut off the fuel (close the gas knob), cut off the air (a lid, a blanket, sand, CO₂), or cool it below its ignition temperature (water).

Substances that can burn are combustible (paper, wood, kerosene); those that cannot are non-combustible (stone, glass, sand, iron). Substances with a very low ignition temperature that catch fire easily from a flame or spark, such as petrol, alcohol and LPG, are called inflammable.

🧮 The reactions

C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat and light (charcoal, and the carbon in wood) CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat (methane, the main gas in CNG and biogas) C₄H₁₀ (butane, in LPG) + oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O + heat: 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O

Burning releases heat, and that heat keeps the next bit of fuel above its ignition temperature. This is why a fire, once started, keeps itself going.

Approximate ignition temperatures used here (published values vary with the sample, its size and how it is heated): paper about 230 °C, dry wood about 300 °C, plastic wire covering about 450 °C, kerosene about 220 °C by heat alone, LPG about 450 °C by heat alone. Kerosene gives off enough vapour to catch fire from a nearby flame above its flash point, about 50 °C (real kerosene: roughly 38–72 °C). LPG catches from a flame or spark at any normal temperature.

Simplified: the fire is a toy model. The temperature, oxygen and fuel move smoothly towards their targets at made-up speeds chosen to be easy to watch; “temperature of the fuel” is one number for the whole fuel; flames go out below 15% oxygen; the paper cup holds enough water to last about a minute and a half on the hottest plate. Real fires are far less tidy, and smoke, toxic gases and spreading make them much more dangerous.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • LPG at home: the gas has a strong smell added so a leak is noticed. If you smell gas, close the cylinder regulator, open doors and windows, and do not light a match or touch electric switches (a switch makes a tiny spark).
  • Oil in a kadhai catches fire: turn off the stove and slide a lid over it. Never throw water: it sinks under the hot oil, boils at once and throws burning oil out.
  • Electrical fires: switch off the mains (MCB) first. Water conducts electricity, so it can give a deadly shock.
  • Fire extinguishers in school buses, schools, cinemas and offices are often CO₂ or dry powder types. CO₂ is heavier than air, so it covers the fire like a blanket, and it is safe on oil and electrical fires.
  • Forest fires in summer, for example in Uttarakhand, spread through dry leaves and twigs. Fire lines (cleared strips with no fuel) stop them by removing fuel.
  • Diwali crackers contain fuel and chemicals that supply their own oxygen, so they must be lit from a distance, with a bucket of water or sand nearby.
  • In an emergency in India, call the fire service on 101 (or 112).

🎯 Try this

  1. With Paper as the fuel, press Light it, then press Cover with a lid / blanket. Watch the oxygen bar. At what oxygen level does the flame go out, and which side of the triangle turns grey?
    Show answerThe fire uses up the oxygen under the lid. When the oxygen near the fuel falls below about 15% the flame dies, and the OXYGEN side goes grey. Fuel and heat were still there for a while.
  2. Choose Kerosene in a dish, light it and press Pour water. What happens? Then put it out the right way.
    Show answerThe fire gets bigger and burning oil splashes out. Water is heavier than kerosene and does not mix with it, so it sinks, boils and throws the oil about. A lid, sand or the CO₂ extinguisher works, because they cut off the air.
  3. Choose the LPG gas stove, light it and press Throw sand. Why is the message red even though the flame went out? What should you have done?
    Show answerThe flame is out but the knob is still open, so gas keeps leaking into the room, where a spark could make it explode. For a gas fire, close the knob first: that removes the fuel and the flame dies at once.
  4. Choose Electrical fire, light it and try Pour water. Then try the CO₂ extinguisher without switching off the mains, and wait. What happens, and what is the safe order?
    Show answerWater is refused: it conducts electricity and could give a shock. CO₂ puts the flames out, but the sparking plug keeps heating the plastic above 450 °C, so it catches fire again when the CO₂ drifts away. Safe order: switch off the mains, then use CO₂ or sand.
  5. Change the Scene to the ignition temperature test, set the hot plate to 350 °C and press Light it. Which items catch fire, and why does the paper cup with water not burn? Then try 500 °C with a lit match held near.
    Show answerAt 350 °C paper (about 230 °C), kerosene (about 220 °C) and, a little later, wood (about 300 °C) catch fire; plastic and LPG need about 450 °C, so they do not. The water in the cup boils at 100 °C and takes up the heat, so the paper cup stays far below 230 °C. With the match held near, LPG lights at once and kerosene lights once it is warmer than about 50 °C: inflammable vapours catch from a flame long before they reach their ignition temperature by heat alone.

📖 Key terms

  • Combustion: a chemical reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen to give off heat (and often light).
  • Ignition temperature: the lowest temperature at which a substance catches fire.
  • Combustible / non-combustible: can burn / cannot burn.
  • Inflammable: catches fire very easily from a flame or spark because it has a low ignition temperature (petrol, alcohol, LPG).
  • Flash point: the lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off enough vapour to catch fire from a nearby flame.
  • Fire extinguisher: a device that puts out a fire by cutting off air, cooling the fuel, or both.
