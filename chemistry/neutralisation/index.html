📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

An acid and a base cancel each other's effect. When they react, they make a salt and water, and heat is given out. This reaction is called neutralisation.

Here 10 mL of dilute hydrochloric acid sits in a conical flask with a few drops of an indicator. Dilute sodium hydroxide is added from a burette. As long as some acid is left over, the solution stays acidic. When exactly enough base has been added, the solution is neutral and contains only common salt (sodium chloride) and water. One more drop and it turns basic, and the indicator changes colour.

🧮 Key ideas and numbers

acid + base → salt + water (+ heat) hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Both solutions here are equally strong (1 mole per litre), so 10.00 mL of base exactly neutralises 10 mL of acid. Each mL that reacts forms about 58 mg of common salt and gives out about 57 J of heat.

pH (you will meet it in Class 10) is a number for how acidic a solution is: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic. The graph shows how suddenly it jumps near the neutral point: from about 3 to about 11 within two drops.

Simplifications: all the heat stays in the liquid (a real flask loses some), the liquid is treated as water, and the china rose colours are a simple model of a natural dye (real extracts vary). Phenolphthalein turns pink at pH 8.2, just after the neutral point; with these strong solutions that is less than one drop later.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Indigestion: the stomach makes too much hydrochloric acid, and an antacid such as milk of magnesia (a mild base) neutralises the extra acid.
  • Ant sting: it injects formic acid; a paste of baking soda (a base) eases the pain.
  • Factory waste is often acidic. It is neutralised with a base before it is let out into rivers, to protect fish and plants.

🎯 Try this

  1. Open the tap with phenolphthalein and “Close the tap when the colour changes” on. How much base was needed, and what colour did the flask turn?
    Show answerAbout 10 mL (10.05 mL, one drop past neutral): the flask turns pink. Before that, each drop makes a pink patch that disappears as the base meets the acid.
  2. Watch the thermometer as you add base. When is the flask warmest?
    Show answerAt the neutral point, about 33.8 °C from 27 °C. After that no more acid is left to react, and the cool base added only dilutes the warm liquid, so the temperature falls a little.
  3. Swap the flask: put sodium hydroxide in it and add hydrochloric acid. What does phenolphthalein do now?
    Show answerIt starts pink (the flask is basic) and turns colourless near the neutral point.
  4. Use china rose. What colours do you see from start to finish?
    Show answerDark pink (acidic) → pale (neutral) → green (basic).
  5. How would you get the salt out of the neutral solution?
    Show answerEvaporate the water by gently heating it in a china dish. About 0.58 g of common salt stays behind.

📖 Key terms

  • Neutralisation: the reaction between an acid and a base that forms a salt and water and gives out heat.
  • Salt: the substance formed with water when an acid reacts with a base (common salt is one of many salts).
  • Burette: a long glass tube with a scale and a tap, used to add a liquid a little at a time.
  • Phenolphthalein: an indicator that is colourless in acids and pink in bases.
