📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Many compounds, like common salt, are made of ions: atoms or groups of atoms that carry an electric charge. Metals form positive ions (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺); non-metals form negative ions (Cl⁻, O²⁻). The size of the charge is the ion’s valency, its combining capacity.

Think of valency as the number of hands an ion has. In a compound every hand must hold a hand of the opposite kind, so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge and the compound is neutral. Aluminium has 3 hands and oxygen 2, so it takes 2 aluminium ions (6 hands) and 3 oxide ions (6 hands): Al₂O₃.

Some ions are groups of atoms that stay together, called polyatomic ions: ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, sulphate SO₄²⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻. When more than one of them is needed, the group goes in brackets with the number outside: Ca(OH)₂ has one calcium and two whole hydroxide groups.

🧮 Key rules

total positive charge = total negative charge

Number of positive ions × their valency = number of negative ions × their valency.

criss-cross: Al³⁺ O²⁻ → Al₂O₃

Write the valency of each ion above its symbol, swap them to become the small numbers (subscripts) of the other ion, and divide by any common factor: Mg²⁺ O²⁻ → Mg₂O₂ → MgO. A subscript of 1 is not written.

formula unit mass = sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms

Al₂O₃ = 2 × 27 + 3 × 16 = 102 u. Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × 16 + 2 × 1 = 74 u. For compounds made of molecules (like H₂O or CO₂) the same sum is called the molecular mass.

Simplified: atomic masses are rounded (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5, the rest whole numbers). Iron and copper can have more than one valency, so the name shows which one: iron(II) is Fe²⁺, iron(III) is Fe³⁺. Not every pair makes a stable everyday compound (for example, silver hydrogencarbonate is not a common substance), and covalent compounds such as water share electrons rather than forming ions, though the same valency rule gives their formulae.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Common salt is sodium chloride, NaCl: one hand each.
  • Limestone, marble and chalk are calcium carbonate, CaCO₃; the Taj Mahal is built of marble.
  • Lime water used to test for carbon dioxide is calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, and it is also used to whitewash walls.
  • Ammonium sulphate, (NH₄)₂SO₄, and calcium phosphate, Ca₃(PO₄)₂, are used in fertilisers. Calcium phosphate is also what makes bones and teeth hard.
  • Alum, used to clean water in many Indian homes, contains aluminium sulphate, Al₂(SO₄)₃.

🎯 Try this

  1. Keep aluminium and oxide. Change the numbers of each ion until no red hand is left. What is the formula?
    Show answer2 aluminium ions and 3 oxide ions: Al₂O₃. Positive charge 2 × 3 = +6, negative charge 3 × 2 = −6. Formula unit mass 2 × 27 + 3 × 16 = 102 u.
  2. Choose magnesium and oxide, and set 2 of each. Is this right? What should you do?
    Show answerThe charges balance (+4 and −4), but 2 : 2 is not the simplest ratio. Divide both by 2: MgO (40 u).
  3. Make calcium hydroxide. Why are there brackets in its formula?
    Show answerIt needs 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 OH⁻: Ca(OH)₂. The brackets show that the whole OH group is taken twice. Writing CaOH₂ would mean one oxygen and two hydrogens, which is wrong.
  4. Work out the formula of ammonium sulphate on paper, then check it with the tiles.
    Show answerNH₄⁺ has 1 hand and SO₄²⁻ has 2, so you need 2 ammonium ions: (NH₄)₂SO₄. Its mass is 2 × 14 + 8 × 1 + 32 + 4 × 16 = 132 u.
  5. Compare iron(II) chloride and iron(III) chloride. How are they different?
    Show answerFe²⁺ needs 2 chloride ions: FeCl₂ (127 u). Fe³⁺ needs 3: FeCl₃ (162.5 u). The same metal with a different valency makes a different compound.

📖 Key terms

  • Valency: the combining capacity of an atom or ion; for an ion it equals the size of its charge.
  • Cation / anion: a positively / negatively charged ion.
  • Polyatomic ion: a group of atoms that carries a charge and acts as one unit, such as SO₄²⁻.
  • Chemical formula: symbols and subscripts that show which elements are in a compound and in what ratio.
  • Formula unit mass: the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in one formula unit of an ionic compound, in atomic mass units (u).
