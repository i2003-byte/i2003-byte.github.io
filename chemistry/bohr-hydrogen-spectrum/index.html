📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Pass electricity through hydrogen gas in a glass tube and it glows pink-red. Look at that glow through a prism and you do not see a rainbow. You see a few sharp coloured lines: red, blue-green, blue and violet, always at exactly the same places. In 1913 Niels Bohr explained why.

  • The electron in a hydrogen atom can only move in certain allowed orbits, numbered n = 1, 2, 3 … Each orbit has a fixed energy. In an allowed orbit the electron does not give out any energy.
  • To move to a higher orbit, the atom must take in exactly the energy difference, for example from a collision in the lamp or by absorbing a photon of just the right energy.
  • When the electron falls to a lower orbit, the energy difference comes out as one photon of light. A bigger fall gives a more energetic photon, which means a shorter wavelength (towards violet and ultraviolet).
  • Because only certain energy differences are possible, only certain wavelengths appear: a line spectrum. Falls that end on n = 2 (the Balmer series) give the visible lines; falls to n = 1 (Lyman) are ultraviolet; falls to n = 3 (Paschen) are infrared.

🧮 Formulas

Eₙ = −13.6 ÷ n² eV rₙ = 0.0529 × n² nm photon energy E = 13.6 × (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) eV wavelength λ = 1240 ÷ E(eV) nm (same as 1/λ = R_H (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²))

Example: the red line, n = 3 → 2. E = 13.6 × (1/4 − 1/9) = 1.89 eV, so λ = 1240 ÷ 1.89 ≈ 656 nm. The energies are negative because the electron is bound: 13.6 eV must be supplied to pull it free from n = 1 (the ionisation energy).

Simplified: orbits are drawn evenly spaced and the energy ladder is squeezed so all levels fit (real radii grow as n², and the top levels crowd together near 0 eV). In the lamp, an excited atom falls to a random lower level each time; in real atoms some jumps are more likely, so real line brightnesses differ. Bohr's model works for hydrogen (and ions with one electron) but not for atoms with more electrons; the later quantum model replaced orbits with orbitals.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • The red 656 nm line (H-alpha) is how astronomers photograph hydrogen gas clouds in space and flares on the Sun.
  • Each element has its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. Helium was first found in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's light, seen during a solar eclipse observed from Guntur in India.
  • Sodium street lamps glow orange-yellow and neon signs glow red for the same reason: electrons falling between fixed levels in those atoms.
  • Dark lines in starlight (absorption) are the same lines in reverse: cooler gas absorbs exactly the photons that its electrons can use to jump up.

🎯 Try this

  1. Switch on the lamp and let it run for a minute. How many lines appear in the visible window, and what colours are they?
    Show answerFive with the top level at 6 or 7: red 656 nm, blue-green 486 nm, blue 434 nm, violet 410 nm and (with level 7) deep violet 397 nm. They are all falls that end on n = 2, the Balmer series.
  2. Set “Jump from” 3 and “Jump to” 2 and press Make this jump. Then try 4 → 2. Which photon has more energy, and which colour is it?
    Show answer3 → 2 gives 1.89 eV, red (656 nm). 4 → 2 gives 2.55 eV, blue-green (486 nm). The bigger fall gives more energy and a shorter wavelength.
  3. Make the jump 2 → 1. Why can't you see this photon?
    Show answerIt carries 10.2 eV, with a wavelength of 122 nm. That is ultraviolet, far shorter than violet light (about 400 nm), so our eyes cannot see it. All falls to n = 1 (Lyman series) are ultraviolet.
  4. Turn the lamp off. With the electron in n = 1, tap level 3. What must the atom take in? Now tap level 1 again.
    Show answerIt must absorb a photon of exactly 12.09 eV (103 nm, ultraviolet) to rise from n = 1 to n = 3. Falling straight back gives out the same 12.09 eV photon. Absorbing and emitting use the same energy differences.
  5. Lower “Highest level reached” to 3. Which lines are left in the whole spectrum?
    Show answerOnly three: 3 → 2 (red, 656 nm), 3 → 1 (103 nm, UV) and 2 → 1 (122 nm, UV). A low lamp voltage cannot lift electrons higher, so the other lines never appear.

📖 Key terms

  • Stationary state (orbit): an allowed orbit in which the electron has a fixed energy and gives out no radiation.
  • Ground state: the lowest level, n = 1. Higher levels are excited states.
  • Photon: a packet of light energy, E = hν = hc ÷ λ.
  • Emission spectrum: the bright lines given out by a hot or excited gas; an absorption spectrum shows the same lines as dark gaps in a rainbow.
  • Electron-volt (eV): the energy one electron gains across 1 volt, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
