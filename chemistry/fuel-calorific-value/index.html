📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A fuel is a substance we burn to get heat. Different fuels give very different amounts of heat. The heat released when 1 kg of a fuel burns completely is its calorific value, measured in kilojoules per kilogram (kJ/kg). LPG gives about 55 000 kJ/kg, cow dung cakes only about 6 000–8 000 kJ/kg. So to boil the same pot you need a few grams of LPG but hundreds of grams of dung cakes.

Not all of that heat reaches the food. An open chulha lets most of it escape into the air around the pot, while a gas burner puts a much larger share into the pot. This share is the stove's efficiency. A good fuel and a good stove together save fuel, money and forests.

An ideal fuel is easy to get, cheap, burns easily in air at a moderate, steady rate, has a high calorific value and leaves little smoke or residue. No fuel is perfect, so we choose the best one for the job.

Burning fuels also pollutes. Incomplete burning makes carbon monoxide (CO), a poisonous gas that can kill in a closed room, and black soot that harms the lungs. All carbon fuels give carbon dioxide (CO₂), which traps heat and adds to global warming. Coal and diesel give sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NO₂), which cause acid rain. Hydrogen burns to give only water.

🧮 The formulas

Heat needed by the water: Q = m × c × ΔT Mass of fuel burned = Q ÷ (calorific value × efficiency)

Here m is the mass of water (1 L of water has a mass of 1 kg), c = 4.18 kJ/kg °C is the specific heat of water and ΔT is the rise in temperature.

Worked example (1 L of water on LPG): Q = 1 kg × 4.18 kJ/kg °C × (100 − 25) °C = 313.5 kJ. With an ideal stove the LPG needed is 313.5 ÷ 55 000 = 0.0057 kg = 5.7 g. A real burner puts about 58 % of the heat into the pot, so it needs 313.5 ÷ (55 000 × 0.58) ≈ 9.8 g of LPG.

Simplified: each fuel uses one value from its range: cow dung cakes 7 000, wood 20 000, coal 30 000, biogas 37 500, kerosene, petrol and diesel 45 000, CNG (methane) 50 000, LPG 55 000 and hydrogen 150 000 kJ/kg. Stove efficiencies are typical rough values (chulha 10–12 %, coal angithi 20 %, kerosene stove 45 %, gas burners 55–60 %), as are the ash amounts. The model ignores the heat taken by the pot itself and water lost as steam before boiling. Time runs 30 times faster than real time; the clock shows real minutes. Petrol and diesel are vehicle fuels and are shown on a stove only to compare.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Smoke from chulhas burning wood, dung cakes and crop waste is a major cause of lung and eye disease among women and children in villages. Schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (from 2016) gave LPG connections to millions of poor homes to replace that smoke.
  • After a Supreme Court order, Delhi switched its buses and autorickshaws to CNG around 2002. CNG burns much more cleanly than diesel, with far less soot and sulphur dioxide.
  • A biogas plant turns cow dung into a clean gas fuel, and the slurry left behind is good manure. It is a better use of dung than burning cakes of it.
  • Hydrogen has the highest calorific value of all and gives only water, so it is used as a rocket fuel and is being tried for buses, but it is costly to make and hard to store safely.
  • Coal-burning power plants release SO₂ and soot. Newer plants fit equipment that removes much of the SO₂ from the smoke before it leaves the chimney.

📖 Key terms

  • Fuel: a substance that is burned to give heat (and often light).
  • Calorific value: the heat given by the complete burning of 1 kg of a fuel, in kJ/kg.
  • Efficiency (of a stove): the share of the fuel's heat that actually reaches the pot.
  • Ideal fuel: cheap, easy to get, burns at a steady rate, high calorific value, little pollution or residue.
  • Incomplete combustion: burning with too little air, which makes carbon monoxide and soot.
  • Acid rain: rain made acidic by sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels.
