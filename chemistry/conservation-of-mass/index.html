📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

In a chemical reaction, atoms are not created or destroyed. They only change partners to make new substances. So the total mass of the products is always equal to the total mass of the reactants. This is the law of conservation of mass, shown by Antoine Lavoisier in the 1770s.

It can look as if mass changes. When baking soda fizzes in vinegar in an open bottle, carbon dioxide gas escapes, so the balance reading goes down. When magnesium burns in an open crucible, it takes oxygen from the air, so the ash is heavier than the ribbon. Close the container so that nothing can get in or out, and the reading stays exactly the same.

A compound also always contains its elements in the same proportion by mass, however it is made and wherever it comes from. Water from a river, a well or a laboratory is always 1 part hydrogen to 8 parts oxygen by mass. If you mix the elements in any other ratio, the extra is simply left over. This is the law of constant (definite) proportions, stated by Joseph Proust. John Dalton explained both laws with his atomic theory: matter is made of atoms of fixed mass that join in whole numbers.

🧮 Key rules

mass of reactants = mass of products

2.08 g barium chloride + 1.42 g sodium sulphate → 2.33 g barium sulphate + 1.17 g sodium chloride. Both sides add up to 3.50 g.

balance change = gas taken from the air − gas that escaped

0.84 g baking soda gives 0.44 g carbon dioxide; in an open bottle the reading falls by 0.44 g. 0.24 g magnesium takes 0.16 g oxygen from the air; in an open crucible the reading rises by 0.16 g.

water: hydrogen : oxygen = 1 : 8 · magnesium oxide: Mg : O = 3 : 2 · carbon dioxide: C : O = 3 : 8

These come from the atomic masses: H = 1, O = 16, Mg = 24, C = 12. In H₂O there are 2 × 1 = 2 g of hydrogen for every 16 g of oxygen, which is 1 : 8.

Simplified: each reaction uses 0.01 mol of the limiting substance, molar masses are rounded to two decimal places, and the reactions go to completion. Real burning magnesium loses a little white smoke unless the crucible lid is lifted only now and then. A sealed bottle of gas pushes up on the air around it a little more as it swells; this tiny buoyancy effect is ignored. The balance reads to 0.01 g.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • A burning candle seems to disappear, but the wax has turned into carbon dioxide and water vapour that float away. Collect them all and the mass is the same.
  • An iron nail gets heavier as it rusts, because oxygen and water from the air join the iron.
  • When dough rises and bakes, carbon dioxide from baking soda or yeast escapes, so a cake weighs a little less than the batter.
  • Chemists in industry use fixed proportions to mix exactly enough of each raw material, for example in making ammonia for fertilisers, so nothing expensive is wasted.

🎯 Try this

  1. Run “Barium chloride + sodium sulphate” in an open flask. What happens to the balance reading? Why doesn’t it matter whether the flask is closed?
    Show answerA white solid (barium sulphate) appears, but the reading stays at 144.90 g. No gas is made or used, so nothing leaves or enters even when the flask is open.
  2. Run “Baking soda + vinegar” open, then closed. How much does the reading change each time?
    Show answerOpen: it falls by 0.44 g, the mass of carbon dioxide that escapes. Closed: it does not change at all, because the gas is trapped in the bottle.
  3. Burn the magnesium ribbon in the open crucible. Does the ash weigh more or less than the ribbon? Then try the sealed flask.
    Show answerIn the open crucible the reading rises by 0.16 g: 0.24 g magnesium joins with 0.16 g oxygen from the air to make 0.40 g magnesium oxide. In the sealed flask the oxygen was already inside, so the reading does not change.
  4. Choose “hydrogen + oxygen → water”, with 2 g hydrogen and 20 g oxygen. How much water forms and what is left over? Now try 4 g hydrogen.
    Show answer2 g hydrogen uses 16 g oxygen and makes 18 g water; 4 g oxygen is left over. With 4 g hydrogen, the 20 g oxygen is used up first: it reacts with only 2.5 g hydrogen to make 22.5 g water and 1.5 g hydrogen is left.
  5. Using “magnesium + oxygen”, find a pair of masses that leaves nothing over.
    Show answerAny pair in the ratio 3 : 2, for example 6 g magnesium and 4 g oxygen (10 g magnesium oxide), or 3 g and 2 g, or 9 g and 6 g.

📖 Key terms

  • Law of conservation of mass: mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Law of constant proportions: a chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass.
  • Closed system: a container that lets nothing in or out, so all the reactants and products stay on the balance.
  • Precipitate: an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions react.
  • Limiting reactant: the substance that runs out first and decides how much product forms.
