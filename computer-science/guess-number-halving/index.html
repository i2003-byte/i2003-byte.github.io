📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

The computer hides a number and only answers "too high", "too low" or "correct". After every answer, the numbers still possible form one stretch of the number line. The best plan is to guess the middle of that stretch: whatever the answer, half of the possible numbers are ruled out. This is halving, the idea behind binary search.

Guessing one by one (1, 2, 3 …) rules out only one number per guess. Guessing at random in the possible stretch is somewhere in between: sometimes lucky, often slow.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

After k halvings, about N ÷ 2ᵏ numbers are left Guesses needed by halving, at most: the smallest k with 2ᵏ ≥ N + 1, written ⌈log₂(N + 1)⌉ N = 10 → 4 · N = 100 → 7 · N = 1,000 → 10 · N = 10 lakh → 20 One by one: up to N guesses
  • 2¹⁰ = 1,024, so 10 halvings shrink 1,000 numbers to 1. Going from 1,000 to 10,00,000 (a thousand times more) needs only 10 more guesses, because 2¹⁰ ≈ 1,000.
  • Each halving guess gives one bit of information: "upper half" or "lower half". 20 bits are enough to pick one number out of 10 lakh.

Simplification: the computer always answers truthfully, and every guess counts the same.

🌍 In real life

  • The game "20 questions": 20 good yes/no questions can tell apart about 10 lakh different things.
  • An optician narrowing down your lens power ("better with this one or this one?") is halving in steps.
  • A computer finding a square root or solving an equation by "bisection" halves the range where the answer lies.
  • Finding the faulty bulb in a long chain of festival lights: test the middle first, then the half that fails.

📖 Key terms

  • Halving (bisection): splitting the possible range in two equal parts with each test.
  • Logarithm base 2 (log₂ N): how many times N can be halved before reaching 1.
  • Bit: one yes/no answer; k bits can tell apart 2ᵏ possibilities.
