📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

A contour map shows the land from above. A cross-section (or profile) shows it from the side, as if you sliced the land with a giant knife along a line and looked at the cut face. Geographers draw one to see the real shape of the hills and valleys a map is describing.

To draw it by hand, lay a strip of paper along the line from A to B and mark each place where it crosses a contour, with that contour’s height. Then move the strip to a graph, put each mark at its height and join the dots with a smooth curve. The pink dots in the simulation are exactly those marks.

Contours also tell you the type of landform. Where they bend into a V, look at which way the V points. In a valley, the V points uphill, towards higher contours and up the river. On a spur (a ridge sticking out from a hill), the V points downhill, away from the hilltop. Between two hilltops, a low point on the ridge is a saddle.

🧮 Key rules

ground distance = map distance × scale

On this map 1 km of ground is the length of the scale bar. The whole square is 5 km across.

average gradient = height difference ÷ ground distance, written “1 in N”

Falling 80 m over 2 km (2,000 m) is 2000 ÷ 80 = 1 in 25: you go down 1 m for every 25 m you walk.

vertical exaggeration = vertical scale ÷ horizontal scale

Hills are small compared with the distances between them, so profiles are stretched upwards to show their shape. “× 10” means the heights are drawn 10 times bigger than the distances along the ground. The stretch shown depends on the line and on your screen.

Simplified: the land is a made-up 5 km square built from smooth shapes, not a real toposheet. For the line of sight, an eye 2 m above A looks at a point 2 m above B in a straight line; the Earth’s curve is ignored over such short distances.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • In Class 9 and Class 11 practical geography you read Survey of India toposheets at 1 : 50,000. Their contour interval is 20 m, and drawing a cross-section between two points is a common exam question.
  • Army and forest watch-towers are placed where the line of sight is clear over many kilometres. Mobile phone towers too: a ridge between a tower and a village can leave the village with a weak signal.
  • Dams, like the Bhakra dam on the Sutlej, are built where the valley’s cross-section is narrow and deep, so a short wall holds back a lot of water.
  • Hill villages in Uttarakhand and Himachal are often on spurs: the ground is drier, safer from floods and gets more sun than the valley floor.

📖 Key terms

  • Cross-section (profile): a side view of the land along a line drawn on a map.
  • Spur: a ridge of high ground sticking out from a hill into lower land.
  • Valley: low land between hills, often with a river; its contours form Vs pointing upstream.
  • Saddle (col): a dip on a ridge between two hilltops.
  • Spot height: a dot with the exact height of that point, often on a hilltop.
  • Intervisibility: whether two places can see each other, or whether high ground is in the way.
