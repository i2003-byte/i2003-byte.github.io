📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Sunlight arrives at the Earth as parallel rays. When the sun is high, a beam of sunlight falls almost straight down and its energy lands on a small patch of ground. When the sun is low, the same beam hits the ground at a slant and is spread over a longer strip, so each square metre gets less. Shine a torch straight down on a table, then tilt it: the spot gets bigger and dimmer.

A low sun’s beam also travels a longer path through the air. Air, dust and water vapour soak up and scatter part of the beam on the way, so even less reaches the ground. Both effects make mornings, evenings, winters and places far from the equator cooler.

How high the noon sun climbs depends on latitude and the season. Because the Earth’s axis is tilted by about 23.4°, the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer on 21 June and at the Tropic of Capricorn on 21 December. North of the Tropic of Cancer, as in Delhi or Srinagar, the sun is never straight overhead.

The sunshine reaching the ground (called insolation) also depends on the sky. Hot deserts like the Thar and the Sahara lie at about 20–30° from the equator, where dry air sinks from high up and clouds rarely form. Under their clear skies the ground gets more sunshine in a day than the cloudy, rainy equator, which is part of why they are the hottest places on Earth.

🧮 Key rules

noon sun height = 90° − (gap between your latitude and the latitude where the sun is overhead)

The sun is overhead at 23.4° N on 21 June, at the equator at the equinoxes (21 March, 23 September) and at 23.4° S on 21 December. At Jaisalmer (27° N) the noon sun is about 86° high in June but only about 40° high in December.

ground covered by a 1 m wide beam = 1 ÷ sin(sun height)

At 90° the beam covers 1 m; at 30° it covers 2 m, so each square metre gets half as much; at 10° it covers almost 6 m. On a slope, use the angle between the sun and the slope instead of the horizon.

air crossed ≈ 1 ÷ sin(sun height) (sun overhead = 1)

With clear, dry air about 70 % of the direct beam gets through one thickness of air. Two thicknesses let through 0.7 × 0.7 ≈ 49 %, three about 34 %.

Simplified: the sun’s energy at the top of the air is taken as 1361 W/m² all year; a fixed share of the beam gets through each thickness of air (70 % for clear dry air, 62 % for hazy air); a fifth of the lost light comes back as scattered sky light; clouds are a rough average for a monsoon sky. Times are sun time. The close-up is drawn in one flat plane (the beam always comes in from the south side) with the true angle between the sun and the ground, and the layer of air is drawn far thinner than the ground scale would make it. Real totals are a little lower because of dust and haze: Jaisalmer gets about 7 kWh/m² a day in May–June.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Morning sunshine feels gentle and noon sunshine feels harsh, even on the same day: in Jaisalmer in June the sun gives about 280 W on each square metre at 7 am but about 1000 W at noon.
  • Solar panels in India are tilted to face south, by roughly the latitude of the place, so the sun’s rays hit them more squarely through the year.
  • In the Himalayas and in Kashmir, villages and fields are mostly on south-facing slopes, which get far more winter sunshine. North-facing slopes keep their snow longer and are often forested.
  • Winter in north India is cool because the noon sun is low (about 38° in Delhi in December) and the days are short, not because the Earth is further from the sun. The Earth is actually closest to the sun in early January.
  • Near the poles the summer sun never sets, yet it stays low in the sky, so the ice does not melt quickly.

🎯 Try this

  1. Keep the Thar (27°) and 21 June. Set the time to 7 am, then to noon. How many metres does the 1 m beam cover each time, and how much sunlight falls on each square metre?
    Show answerAt 7 am the sun is about 23° high: the beam covers about 2.5 m and crosses about 2.5 thicknesses of air, so only about 280 W/m² arrives. At noon the sun is about 86° high: the beam covers about 1.0 m and about 1030 W/m² arrives, nearly four times as much.
  2. Change the date to 21 December. What happens to the noon sun height and the day’s total sunshine?
    Show answerThe noon sun drops to about 40°, the day shortens to about 10.3 hours, and the day’s total falls from about 8.0 to about 3.3 kWh/m². Lower sun, more spreading, more air and fewer hours together cut the sunshine by more than half.
  3. Set latitude 0 (the equator), 21 March, and the sky to “Cloudy”. Compare the day’s total with the Thar on 21 June under a clear sky.
    Show answerThe cloudy equator gets only about 3 kWh/m² even though the noon sun is overhead, while the clear Thar gets about 8. Clouds reflect most of the direct beam. That is why the hottest places are the clear-sky deserts near the tropics, not the equator.
  4. Set Srinagar (34°), 21 December, noon. Try a slope of +30 (facing south), then −30 (facing north).
    Show answerThe south-facing slope meets the low sun almost head-on (about 690 W/m², and about 3.9 kWh/m² over the day), while the north-facing slope is barely touched (about 100 W/m², about 0.6 kWh/m² over the day). Flat ground is in between. Snow lasts on north-facing slopes.
  5. Set latitude 80 and 21 June, then press “Run the day”. Does the sun set? Is the day’s total bigger than at the equator?
    Show answerThe sun never sets (24 hours of daylight), but it stays only 33° high at noon and about 13° at midnight. The day’s total (about 6.9 kWh/m²) is close to the clear equator’s 7.2 in March, but spread over 24 low-sun hours, so it never feels strong.

📖 Key terms

  • Insolation: incoming solar radiation, the sunshine that reaches the Earth’s surface.
  • Angle of incidence: the angle at which the sun’s rays meet the ground. The more slanting the rays, the less energy each square metre gets.
  • Tropic of Cancer: latitude 23.4° N, the furthest north the sun is ever overhead (on about 21 June). It passes through eight Indian states.
  • Air mass: how much air a sunbeam crosses, compared with the air above you when the sun is overhead.
  • W/m² and kWh/m²: watts per square metre measure sunlight right now; kilowatt-hours per square metre add it up over a day.
