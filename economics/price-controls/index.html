📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

Sometimes the government does not let the market set the price. A price ceiling is a legal maximum price, used to keep essential goods affordable for poor families. A price floor is a legal minimum price, used to protect sellers such as farmers. A control only matters if it is binding: a ceiling below the equilibrium price, or a floor above it. A binding ceiling makes buyers want more and sellers offer less, so there is a shortage. People queue, and some wheat is sold secretly at a higher black-market price. A binding floor makes sellers offer more and buyers want less, so there is a surplus that someone must buy or store. In India the government handles both: ration shops (the Public Distribution System) sell grain below the market price, and the Minimum Support Price (MSP) with government procurement keeps prices up for farmers. The grain bought at MSP is what the ration shops sell.

🧮 Key formulas & rules

Qd = 520 − 10P · Qs = 20P − 140 (tonnes per day) Equilibrium: 520 − 10P = 20P − 140 → P* = ₹22 per kg, Q* = 300 t ceiling Pc < P*: shortage = Qd(Pc) − Qs(Pc) black-market price Pb: Qd(Pb) = Qs(Pc) → Pb = (520 − Qs) ÷ 10 floor Pf > P*: surplus = Qs(Pf) − Qd(Pf) · cost of buying it = surplus × Pf

Simplification: one wheat market with straight-line curves and invented numbers. The black-market price assumes the scarce wheat goes to whoever will pay most. Real MSP operations also involve storage, transport, the export market and state-wise differences.

🌍 In real life

  • Ration shops under the National Food Security Act sell wheat and rice to eligible families at a small fraction of the market price.
  • Every year the government announces MSPs for more than twenty crops. The Food Corporation of India buys wheat and rice at MSP, especially in Punjab, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh, and stores huge buffer stocks.
  • Other examples: rent control in old city buildings (a ceiling, often with too few flats to rent) and minimum wages (a floor on the price of labour).

🎯 Try this

  1. Set a price ceiling of ₹16. How big is the shortage? What would buyers pay on the black market?
    Show answerBuyers want 360 t but farmers offer only 180 t: a shortage of 180 t per day. For just 180 t, the last buyer would pay ₹34 per kg, far above both the ceiling and the free-market ₹22.
  2. Keep the ceiling and turn on "Government steps in". What changes?
    Show answerRation shops supply the missing 180 t per day from government stocks, so all 360 t reach buyers at ₹16 and the black market disappears. The cost is that the government must first have that grain in store.
  3. Now move the ceiling up to ₹25. What happens?
    Show answerNothing: the ceiling is above the equilibrium price, so the market simply trades at ₹22. The ceiling is not binding.
  4. Choose the MSP example (floor ₹26) with the government buying. How much does procurement cost per day? Then raise the floor to ₹30.
    Show answerAt ₹26 the surplus is 120 t, costing ₹31.2 lakh a day. At ₹30 the surplus is 240 t and the cost is ₹72 lakh a day: a small rise in the floor more than doubles the bill, because farmers grow more and buyers buy less.
  5. Turn the government off at the ₹26 floor. Why is that a problem for farmers?
    Show answerBuyers take only 260 t, so 120 t a day stays unsold. Farmers with unsold grain are tempted to sell below the floor, so the floor breaks down unless the government buys.

📖 Key terms

  • Price ceiling: the highest price the law allows.
  • Price floor: the lowest price the law allows. MSP works as a floor for crops the government buys.
  • Binding: a control that actually changes the market price.
  • Black market: illegal trade at a price above the ceiling.
  • Buffer stock: grain the government stores to supply ration shops and steady prices.
