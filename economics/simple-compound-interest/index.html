📘 Learn the science

💡 The big idea

When you keep money in a bank, the bank pays you interest for using it. When you borrow, you pay interest to the lender. There are two ways to count it.

  • Simple interest: interest is worked out on the original amount (the principal) only. The same amount is added every year, so the bars grow in equal steps.
  • Compound interest: each time interest is added, it joins the principal, and next time it earns interest too. This interest on interest (orange in the bars) is small at first, then grows faster and faster.
  • Banks compound interest on fixed deposits and most loans, so the compound bar is the one that usually matters in real life.

🧮 Formulas

SI = P × R × T ÷ 100, A = P + SI A = P × (1 + R ÷ 100)^T (compounded yearly) A = P × (1 + R ÷ 100k)^(k × T) (added k times a year) time to double ≈ 72 ÷ R years (compound) · 100 ÷ R years (simple)

Example: ₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years. Simple: 10,000 + 2,000 = ₹12,000. Compound yearly: 10,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = ₹12,100. The extra ₹100 is the 10% interest earned on the first year's ₹1,000 of interest.

Simplified: one fixed rate for the whole time, no tax on interest, no repayments and no fees. For a loan, the picture shows what you would owe if you paid nothing back; with equal monthly payments (EMIs) the amount owed falls instead. Compound interest is added only at the end of each period, so the bars and graph rise in steps.

🌍 Real-world examples

  • Bank fixed deposits in India usually compound interest every quarter. A 7% FD then grows like 7.19% a year (the “effective yearly rate” readout).
  • Some village moneylenders charge 3% to 5% a month, which is 36% to 60% a year. At 36% compounded, a ₹10,000 loan becomes about ₹18,500 in 2 years and over ₹46,000 in 5 years if nothing is paid back. This is how farmers fall into debt traps, and why formal credit from banks, cooperatives and self-help groups matters.
  • Credit card dues that are not paid in full are charged around 3–3.75% a month, compounded, one of the most expensive kinds of credit.
  • The rule of 72 is a quick mental check: at 8% a year, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years.

📖 Key terms

  • Principal: the amount first deposited or borrowed.
  • Rate of interest: the interest on ₹100 for one year, written as % per annum (p.a.).
  • Compounding period: how often interest is added to the principal (yearly, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly).
  • Effective annual rate: the yearly rate that would give the same growth if interest were added only once a year.
  • Collateral: an asset (land, gold) that a borrower pledges to the lender until the loan is repaid.
